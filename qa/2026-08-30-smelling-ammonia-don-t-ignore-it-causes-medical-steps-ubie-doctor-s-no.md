# Smelling Ammonia? Don’t Ignore It! Causes & Medical Steps | Ubie Doctor's Note

Published on: 4/13/2026

Smelling Ammonia? Why Your Body Smells & Medically Approved Next Steps

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

An ammonia-like smell from breath, sweat, or urine is most commonly caused by dehydration or a high-protein, low-carb diet. However, it can also signal urinary tract infections (UTIs), kidney disease, or liver dysfunction. A fruity odor sometimes mistaken for ammonia may indicate uncontrolled diabetes (diabetic ketoacidosis), which requires urgent medical care.

First steps include hydrating well and rebalancing your diet with more carbohydrates. Seek medical care if the smell persists beyond a few days, or if you experience burning during urination, fever, swelling, jaundice, confusion, or severe fatigue. Clinicians may order a urinalysis along with kidney, liver, and blood glucose tests to guide treatment.

Because an ammonia-like odor can stem from harmless causes or signal something serious, identifying your specific pattern of symptoms is essential before deciding on next steps. What Is Ammonia and Why Does It Smell?

Ammonia is a waste product your body naturally produces when it breaks down protein. Your liver normally converts ammonia into urea, which your kidneys remove through urine.

But when ammonia builds up—or when it becomes more concentrated—you may start to smell it on your breath, in your sweat, or in your urine.

Common Reasons You Smell Ammonia

1. Dehydration (Most Common Cause)

When you're dehydrated:

Your urine becomes concentrated

Waste products, including ammonia, become stronger

The smell becomes sharper and more noticeable

You may notice:

Dark yellow urine

Strong urine odor

Dry mouth

Headache or fatigue

What to do:Drink more fluids. For most adults, that means enough water so your urine stays pale yellow. If the smell improves after hydration, dehydration was likely the cause.

2. High-Protein Diet

If you eat a lot of protein (keto, carnivore, bodybuilding diets), your body breaks down more amino acids. That process produces more ammonia.

Signs this may be diet-related:

You recently increased protein intake

You're following a low-carb or ketogenic diet

You notice ammonia smell after intense workouts

Athletes sometimes notice ammonia-smelling sweat, especially during long workouts when glycogen (stored carbs) runs low and the body shifts to protein breakdown.

What to do:

Increase hydration

Add moderate healthy carbohydrates

Avoid extreme protein intake unless medically supervised

3. Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)

A strong ammonia smell in urine can sometimes signal a urinary tract infection.

Other symptoms may include:

Burning when urinating

Frequent urge to urinate

Cloudy urine

Pelvic discomfort

Fever (in more serious cases)

UTIs are common and treatable, but they should not be ignored.

What to do:If you have urinary symptoms along with ammonia odor, speak to a doctor promptly.

4. Kidney Problems

Your kidneys are responsible for removing waste, including ammonia (via urea). If they're not functioning properly, waste can build up.

Possible warning signs include:

Swelling in legs or feet

Fatigue

Foamy urine

Changes in urination frequency

Persistent ammonia-like breath

Kidney disease often develops gradually and may not cause early symptoms. That's why persistent odor changes should not be ignored.

What to do:If the smell continues without a clear cause, ask your doctor about kidney function testing.

5. Liver Problems (More Serious)

The liver converts ammonia into urea. If the liver isn't working properly, ammonia levels can rise in the bloodstream.

In advanced liver disease, high ammonia can cause:

Confusion

Personality changes

Drowsiness

A musty or ammonia-like breath odor

This condition (hepatic encephalopathy) is serious and requires immediate medical care.

What to do:If ammonia smell is accompanied by confusion, severe fatigue, yellowing of skin/eyes, or abdominal swelling, seek urgent medical attention.

6. Diabetes or Metabolic Conditions

Sometimes people confuse an ammonia smell with a fruity or acetone smell, which may be related to uncontrolled diabetes and a condition called diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

DKA is a medical emergency and may include:

Fruity breath odor

Rapid breathing

Nausea and vomiting

Severe fatigue

7. Rare Genetic Conditions

Certain inherited metabolic disorders can interfere with ammonia processing. These are usually diagnosed in infancy or childhood, but mild forms can appear later.

These are uncommon but may be considered if symptoms are persistent and unexplained.

When to See a Doctor

Occasional ammonia smell that improves with hydration is usually not dangerous.

However, see a doctor if you notice:

Persistent ammonia odor lasting more than a few days

Burning or pain with urination

Fever

Swelling in legs

Yellowing of eyes or skin

Confusion or personality changes

Symptoms of uncontrolled diabetes

Medically Approved Next Steps

Here's a practical plan you can follow:

Step 1: Hydrate

Drink enough water to keep urine pale yellow

Increase fluids if exercising or in hot weather

Limit excessive caffeine and alcohol

Step 2: Review Your Diet

Avoid extreme high-protein intake

Balance protein with healthy carbohydrates

Consider speaking to a dietitian if on keto or bodybuilding diets

Step 3: Watch for Additional Symptoms

Monitor for:

Pain with urination

Swelling

If additional symptoms appear, seek medical care.

Step 4: Request Basic Lab Work (If Persistent)

A doctor may order:

Urinalysis

Kidney function tests (creatinine, BUN)

Liver function tests

Blood glucose levels

These tests are straightforward and can rule out serious causes.

How to Reduce Ammonia Smell

Depending on the cause, you may improve odor by:

Drinking more water

Showering after exercise

Wearing breathable clothing

Improving hygiene

Treating infections promptly

Managing chronic conditions properly

If body odor itself is your main concern, addressing underlying causes is far more effective than simply masking it.

The Bottom Line

Smelling ammonia from your body is usually related to:

Exercise-related metabolism

These causes are common and often easily corrected.

However, persistent ammonia odor—especially when combined with other symptoms—can signal problems involving the kidneys, liver, urinary tract, or metabolism.

Don't panic. But don't ignore it either.

If something feels off, speak to a doctor. Early evaluation is simple and can rule out serious conditions before they progress.
