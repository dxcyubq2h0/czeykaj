So how long does alcohol stay in your system? To make sure you have the right answers, a medical doctor breaks down how alcohol metabolism works.

How Alcohol is Metabolized by the Body

Alcohol metabolism is partially based on a person’s individual alcohol use habits as well as their genetic makeup, says Suneet Singh, MD, an emergency room physician and medical director of CareHive Health in Austin.

“The path alcohol takes from consumption to elimination involves a metabolism process that first starts in the stomach where various enzymes begin the overall breakdown cycle,” he explains. “Most of the alcohol passes unaltered into the small intestine where absorption into the bloodstream takes place, and upon reaching the bloodstream, alcohol is predominantly transported to the liver where over 90% of its metabolism occurs.”

The vast majority of the alcohol you drink is metabolized by your liver. A small amount fully digested with no side effects.

Now once alcohol reaches the liver, it's converted into a chemical called acetaldehyde. You might recognize the substance as the one behind the not so fun effects of alcohol, including headaches, nausea, and heart palpitations.

How Long it Takes to Clear Alcohol From Your Body

Ready for some number crunching? If you're having one standard drink—12 ounces or one can of beer, 5 ounces or one glass of wine, or 1.5 ounces or one shot of 80-proof distilled spirits—Singh says expect for the alcohol to peak in the bloodstream an hour after drinking.

From there, it takes about five half-lives to process and eliminate alcohol from your system completely. Alcohol has a half-life of four to five hours, which means it takes about 20 to 25 hours for it to clear out. In other words, Singh says "it takes about a day for the body to fully clear one serving of alcohol.”

Also, ignore the myth that your body recognizes different liquors differently. Your liver doesn't register a glass of wine any differently from a mixed cocktail—it only processes alcohol. If one drink has a higher alcohol blood volume (ABV) than the other, your liver will have to work harder.

Now there's a reason you don't feel intoxicated for 20 hours from a pint. Singh adds the physical symptoms of drinking alcohol and intoxication resolve much sooner than completion of the overall metabolism cycle.

How Long Alcohol is Detectable in Your System

If you've knocked back a couple of drinks, it makes sense to want to know if you're good to drive or work the next day.

There's a ton of factors to consider when quantifying how long alcohol is detectable in your body. Singh says factors like your genetics, enzymes levels, alcohol quantity, and alcohol strength determine whether you pass a breathalyzer test after a few hours. Singh adds there's also age, body mass, overall health, and the testing method to consider.

Still, doctors have come up with a general ballpark you can use as an estimated reference.

Everyone Metabolizes Alcohol Differently

Two enzymes in the liver, alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), affect the rate your body metabolizes alcohol. Singh says genetic and environmental factors can change how much of these enzymes are present.

“Studies have shown that males tend to have higher amounts of ADH than females, and people who consume alcohol regularly tend to have lower amounts of ADH compared to those who drink less often,” he explains. “Research has also demonstrated that around 35-40 percent of people of East Asian descent have lower amounts of the ALDH compared to other ethnicities."

In short, the fewer alcohol metabolizing enzymes you have, the longer it takes for your body to metabolize alcohol—as well as those unpleasant, physical symptoms like nausea and headaches.

How much you drink and how strong your alcohol of choice is also plays a role in metabolism and enzyme levels, Singh adds. Higher amounts unsurprisingly contribute to longer processing times.

The bottom line: How long it takes alcohol to clear your system, as well as how it can affect you physically, can easily vary by person. Take note of how you tend to feel after drinking any amount of alcohol and take steps to avoid those undesirable short-term effects, as well as the long-term effects that can come with too much alcohol consumption.
