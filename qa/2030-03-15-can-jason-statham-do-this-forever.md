# Can Jason Statham Do This Forever?

SPOILER ALERT FOR A Working Man: Jason Statham plays an unassuming blue collar type who is actually an elite soldier compelled by his moral code to protect the defenseless against the criminal element. By now, Statham can play the “indestructible action hero” part in his sleep, though such is his professionalism that he shows up bright eyed and bushy tailed no matter the gig; If he and director David Ayer have it their way, they’ll probably shoot a movie like this every year, and we’ll probably be happy about it, too.

[Can Jason Statham Do](https://memorycircuits.hbsnzj.com/)

But that “if” is a big “if,” and should be accompanied by a bigger “can.” Yes, Statham has movies like A Working Man down to a vicious science, where he takes on all comers and none of them prove a match for his awesome brawn and scowling mug; whether regular grade scumbags or high level killers, his bone-shattering prowess inevitably overwhelms them all. In many ways, Statham’s body of work makes a refreshing improvement over the star-spangled dreck Steven Segal built his career on, the most important being that Statham has soul and a personality. The depth of compassion his characters express for good, downtrodden people victimized by amoral parties at times seems to be his own; as much as Levon Cade bristles at the suffering of innocents, so does Statham.

Levon is Statham’s hero in A Working Man, a Chicago construction worker seeking out peace of mind, and also just plain old peace, after serving as a Royal Marine for several decades. The change in surroundings suits him; putting up a building in middle America requires assured, decisive leadership, qualities he has in abundance thanks to the time he put in overseas mowing down bad guys and saving his fellow servicemen. As a bonus, no one’s taking sniper fire while pouring concrete, either! Then comes the day a squad of drug traffickers show up and cause trouble at Levon’s job site, forcing him to once again call on his particular set of skills to send them packing using the literal tools of his trade. Remember, for those of you in the cheap seats: he’s a working man.

From there, greasy kidnappers grab Jenny (Arianna Rivas), daughter of his bosses, Joe (Michael Peña) and Carla (Noemi Gonzalez), and the movie becomes the dollar store take on Man on Fire. Joe and Carla beg Levon to recover Jenny; he demurs for the five minutes it takes his best friend and former comrade-at-arms, Gunny (David Harbour), to change his mind; he sets out to pick up Jenny’s trail, leaving no traffickers alive in his relentless, but muddled, hunt. If Ayer mapped out Levon’s search, it would mirror a Billy path from Family Circus; A Working Man is all over the place, making pit stops with the Russian Vory, the cops on their payroll, and a honkytonk biker gang along the way.

While the plot is familiar, so is Statham’s presence, which has a calming effect on the tropes Ayer and his co-writer, Sylvester Stallone himself, deploy in the screenplay. Even in his worst movies, Statham’s taut charisma, communicated by narrowing his eyes and clenching his jaw, remains endlessly watchable. A Working Man falls in the middle of that spectrum; It’s a few steps down from his first collaboration with Ayer, 2024’s The Beekeeper, a rare instance where the product does what it says on the tin, and several steps above similar fare in his filmography (a’la 2013’s Homefront). Despite the middling unwieldiness, the film is suitably diverting. You’re also likely to forget most of its plot as the credits start rolling, in much the same way the movie neglects that Levon has his own daughter, Merry (Isla Gie), while he searches for Jenny. (His purpose is admirable, of course, but the responsibility Levon owes to Merry, plus the responsibility he feels for Jenny, is a bit like putting a hat on a hat.)

But is that how Statham wants his career remembered, and how audiences prefer to see him? Statham certainly has dug a firm foothold in studio action. He may even have been born to lead productions in that ballpark, being physically imposing and intimidating without discarding qualities like “humor” and “amiability.” But movies like Spy, not to mention his team-ups with Guy Ritchie, from earlier (Lock, Stock and Two Smoking Barrels and Snatch) to later (Operation Fortune: Ruse de Guerre), remind us that he tamps down his comic and leading man charm into supplementary traits for his action roles. He’s capable of doing more than kicking a man through a wall, or mowing down scores of anonymous henchmen, though we’re grateful that he’s so good at doing both as he creeps toward 60.

[Can Jason Statham](https://repair-cascade.jcesqc8.com/)

Still, other actors in Statham’s field, like Tom Cruise, get to pivot. If Cruise wants to take a pause on the Mission Impossible series following this year’s The Final Reckoning, he can. In fact, he has; once Cruise tucks the eighth chapter in that franchise under his belt, he’s on to Alejandro González Iñárritu’s untitled next movie, with a release timed for the 2026 awards season. Comparatively, Statham’s future docket includes new movies from Jean-François Richet (Plane) and Ric Roman Waugh (Greenland). No one should shape their career around pursuit of trophies, and it’s admirable that Statham opts for projects that interest him rather than ones that might bring him red carpet; Frankly, we could use more examples of that kind of career building. He and his audience are better off that he sticks with what works for both.

But if Statham ever hits a wall, and movies like A Working Man recede from his reach in terms of physical demand, what will he end up doing with himself? Get back to making a new Spy movie, not necessarily a sequel, but something in that vein, where he can relax his muscles and put his comic timing to the test. There’s more to Statham than we get to see in between his punch barrages on gangsters, lowlifes, and sickos. Maybe he ought to consider working on that.
