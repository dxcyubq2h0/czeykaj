# The 'Stack Dating' Trend Promises To 'Optimize' Your Love Life, But Experts Aren't Convinced

WHEN JOE, 33, first downloaded Tinder at age 21, he’d only been with a few women and wasn't exactly experienced with dating. But within hours of setting up his profile, he matched with someone who lived 15 minutes away. They met for drinks, hit it off, and hooked up. "It was the easiest thing in the world," Joe recalls.

That first successful date set off what Joe calls a "rampage"—years of organizing his dating life like a part-time job. He would even schedule back-to-back dates at the same bar, just two hours apart (a 7 p.m. and a 9 p.m.), with a built-in backup plan. "If the first one works out, then great. I'll just raincheck on the second," he says.

While that might sound like a callous approach to casual dating, it’s not an uncommon one. Many singles are now "stack dating," a term used to describe scheduling multiple first dates in a single day or "stacking" dates onto their existing daily schedule. Think: grabbing coffee with one match during your lunch break, then meeting another for a quick drink after work, all before dinner with your friends. It's also sometimes dubbed "errand dating" because you're basically fitting dates in wherever they naturally work in your day, rather than blocking off entire nights.

The Rise of the 'Stack Dating' Trend

THE CONCEPT OF saving time while looking for love has been around since the days of speed dating events, but this trending technique blew up on TikTok a couple years ago. And Gen Z is all about it. In fact, about half of Gen-Zers are looking for ways to fit dating into their daily schedules, according to research from Tinder's 2023 Future of Dating report, with 32 percent admitting they went on a date during their workday.

Many younger singles have even taken to social media to broadcast their stacking success stories. One TikToker shared how he had a date scheduled and then met a girl at the gym earlier that day, so he pushed up the first date to be able to squeeze in a drink with gym girl afterwards. In another TikTok video, a podcast host shared how his friend once scheduled a date at 7 pm and another at 8:30 pm on the same night—and he ended up marrying the 8:30 date.

But experts aren’t so easily convinced. In an age when daters are increasingly using AI to write their dating app profiles and help them text with matches (another new dating term called “chatfishing”), this technique further “treats dating as something to optimize,” says Shahrzad Jalali, PsyD, a clinical psychologist in Newport Beach, CA. "Where traditional casual dating leaves emotional space for curiosity and connection, stack dating compresses it," she says. “It’s not 'let’s see what unfolds,' but 'let’s see what fits into my schedule.'”

The Pros and Cons of Stack Dating

LIKE PRETTY MUCH all dating trends, stack dating has its pros and cons. But it can work to your favor if you approach each date intentionally, rather than just dating to try to fill a void.

For starters, dating multiple people can help reveal emotional patterns and "function as a mirror, an experiment that shows who you become in different dynamics," Jalali says. There are real advantages, particularly for people who overattach or romanticize pretty quickly.

But when you spend a date half-focused on making the next one, or you're talking to so many people that you forget important details about their lives, then you're probably losing the plot. (Quick reminder: The plot is to find love.)

Let's dive into the good and bad of stack dating so you can figure out if it's an approach worth trying.

The Pros:

It lowers the pressure and gives you perspective: When you’re not putting all your focus on one person, it’s easier to stay relaxed and realistic about where things are going, says NYC-based neuropsychologist Sanam Hafeez, PsyD. "You’re less likely to ignore red flags or rush into something just because it feels exciting. It gives you space to see who actually fits into your life instead of trying to make someone fit." It's also a great reminder that dating is supposed to be about exploring, not proving your worth to someone else.

It can give clarity through contrast: Dating more than one person can highlight patterns, who makes you feel relaxed, who sparks your curiosity, and who drains your energy, says Eli Weinstein, LCSW, host of The Dude Therapist podcast. Seeing those differences side by side helps you figure out what kind of connection feels right.

It reduces premature attachment: "When you’re dating several people, you naturally stop putting all your emotional eggs in one basket," Weinstein says. That can help decrease anxiety, allowing you to show up as your true self rather than performing for an outcome. And Hafeez agrees: "Seeing more than one person helps you avoid overinvesting emotionally in someone before you can tell if you're actually compatible," she says.

It may boost your social confidence: Interacting with different people forces you to communicate better, observe how you show up, and refine how you connect, Hafeez says. "Each date becomes a low-stakes opportunity to practice honesty, curiosity, and boundaries." Think of it like getting reps in the gym — the more you do, the stronger you get. And the same goes for dating. Over time, meeting people can help you feel more relaxed in dating.

It's easy: Fitting a quick coffee date into your existing routine (like, near your gym or on your usual commute route) means you don't have to dedicate an entire evening to a date. You're still putting yourself out there romantically without completely reorganizing your week around someone you just matched with.

The Cons:

It can make it difficult to form a real connection: Stack dates tend to be shorter (say, less than an hour), which minimizes vulnerability, says Jalali. "By layering multiple short encounters, the individual maintains control and reduces emotional exposure," she says. "It’s an efficient system for avoiding rejection or disappointment—but that same efficiency can flatten the depth of human experience."

It might cause you to reject someone prematurely: Stack dating doesn’t really give you much time to get to know the other person before deciding whether you want to go on another date, so you risk writing someone off too soon. "When every interaction is brief and evaluative, people start losing their capacity to feel moved," says Jalali. This eventually can turn into detachment, she adds.

It can create decision paralysis: When you're juggling multiple people at once, it can be hard to really evaluate each person and whether they mirror your values. Weinstein warns against turning dating into a scorecard: "Constantly comparing people or ranking experiences will only mess with your perspective," he says.

It might lead to burnout: Our emotional energy is limited. Meeting new people takes effort, and stack dating can make dating "feel like work instead of connection," Weinstein says. Think about when your day is filled with back-to-back meetings—that’s not the vibe you want to feel in your free time.

How to Make Stack Dating Work for You

STACK DATING SHOULD make your life easier, not turn dating into a second job. You have to get real with yourself and ask if you're scheduling back-to-back dates for the sake of efficiency or because you're dodging vulnerability and deeper feelings.

As you're trying out stack dating (say, you're on your second date of the day), Jalali urges you to ask yourself: "Am I exploring or avoiding?" Stack dating can be valuable if you’re using it to learn about what you want in a long-term partner (like your non-negotiables and what creates chemistry and compatibility). But if you’re using it to keep connections surface-level, it’s likely reinforcing a fear of vulnerability.

If you do go about it, make sure to be upfront with your date and tell them from the get-go that you prefer first dates to be brief. Try something simple like, “I usually like first dates to be short and casual so we can see if there’s a connection,” Hafeez says.

Once you've started stacking, set some boundaries about how many people you date at once. There isn’t a hard-and-fast rule, but most people find that dating two or three people at once in the very beginning stages is manageable without feeling scattered, says Hafeez.

One final word of advice: "If it’s starting to feel like managing a small business rather than exploring connection, it’s time to pause," Weinstein says. Fewer, more meaningful conversations will pay off more than five halfhearted ones.
