Is the Pain Between Your Toes a Neuroma or Diabetes?

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Burning pain between your third and fourth toes may indicate Morton's neuroma, a thickened nerve commonly caused by tight, narrow, or high-heeled shoes. In contrast, gradual burning or tingling in both feet often points to diabetic neuropathy, a result of chronically elevated blood sugar damaging peripheral nerves.

Key factors to evaluate:

Shoe relation: Does pain worsen with certain footwear?

Diabetes history: Do you have elevated blood sugar or diabetes?

Pain onset: Sudden and sharp, or gradual and constant?

Location: Focal (one spot) vs. widespread (both feet)

Diagnostic tests: Physical exam, imaging, or blood glucose testing

Treatment options: Footwear changes, orthotics, blood sugar control, or medication

Explanation

When you feel a sharp, burning, or tingling pain between your toes, it's natural to wonder what's causing it. Two common culprits are Morton's neuroma and diabetic nerve damage (diabetic neuropathy). While these conditions can share some symptoms, they have distinct causes, risk factors, and treatments. Understanding the differences can help you get the right care—and avoid unnecessary anxiety.

What Is Morton's Neuroma?

Morton's neuroma is a thickening of the tissue around one of the nerves leading to your toes, usually between the third and fourth toes. It's not a true tumor but rather an irritated or compressed nerve.

Key features:

Location: Most often between the third and fourth toes.

Pain quality: Burning, stabbing, or electric-shock–like sensations.

Onset: Often triggered by activities that compress the forefoot (e.g., tight shoes, high heels, or running).

Sensations: Numbness or tingling in the toes; feeling of a pebble in the shoe.

High-heeled or narrow shoes

Certain foot shapes (flat feet or high arches)

Repetitive stress on the ball of the foot

What Is Diabetic Neuropathy in the Feet?

Diabetic neuropathy occurs when chronically high blood sugar levels damage the nerves, especially in the hands and feet. This nerve damage can cause a variety of sensations.

Location: Often starts in the toes and feet, then can progress up the legs.

Pain quality: Burning, tingling, "pins and needles," or reduced sensation.

Onset: Gradual, over months to years of uncontrolled blood sugar.

When It Could Be Morton's Neuroma

Consider Morton's neuroma if you notice:

Pain that flares up when wearing tight or narrow shoes.

A feeling like there's something stuck under the ball of your foot.

Pain relieved by removing shoes and massaging the area.

Symptoms confined to a specific nerve path (usually between two toes).

When It Could Be Diabetic Nerve Pain

Diabetic neuropathy is more likely if you have:

A diagnosis of diabetes or elevated blood sugar (check your latest A1C).

Widespread tingling or numbness in both feet or up the legs.

Constant burning pain, even when you're resting.

Other diabetes symptoms: increased thirst, frequent urination, unexplained weight changes.

Because nerve damage from diabetes can lead to serious complications (like foot ulcers), it's crucial to manage your blood sugar and seek professional guidance promptly.

Diagnosing Your Pain

Whether you suspect Morton's neuroma vs diabetic nerve damage, accurate diagnosis usually involves:

Medical history & physical exam

Questions about duration, triggers, and other symptoms

Examination of foot shape, sensation, and reflexes

Imaging and tests

Ultrasound or MRI for neuroma

Nerve conduction studies for neuropathy

Blood tests to check glucose, A1C, vitamin levels

Mulder's click test for neuroma (pressing the foot to reproduce the clicking/pain)

Monofilament test for diabetic nerve loss (light touch sensation)

Treatment Options

For Morton's Neuroma

Footwear changes– Wider, low-heeled shoes with a roomy toe box– Orthotic inserts to relieve pressure

Activity modification– Rest from high-impact activities– Foot mobilization exercises

Medications & injections– Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)– Corticosteroid injections to reduce inflammation

Physical therapy– Toe stretches, massage, ultrasound therapy

Surgery (in severe cases)– Nerve decompression or removal of the neuroma

For Diabetic Neuropathy

Blood sugar control– Diet, exercise, and medications (insulin or oral agents)– Regular monitoring of glucose levels

Pain management– Prescription medications (e.g., gabapentin, duloxetine)– Topical treatments (capsaicin cream)

Foot care– Daily foot inspections for cuts or blisters– Proper footwear to prevent ulcers

Lifestyle measures– Smoking cessation– Management of blood pressure and cholesterol

Referral to specialists– Endocrinologist for diabetes control– Podiatrist for foot ulcer prevention

Early intervention can significantly improve outcomes in both conditions.

Preventing Future Foot Pain

To reduce the risk of developing Morton's neuroma or worsening diabetic nerve pain:

Choose well-fitting, supportive footwear.

Avoid high heels and shoes with a narrow toe box.

Maintain a healthy weight to reduce foot pressure.

Keep blood sugar within target range.

Inspect your feet daily for any cuts, sores, or changes.

Stay active with low-impact exercises (e.g., swimming, cycling).

A proactive approach keeps you moving comfortably and lowers long-term risks.

When to See a Doctor

Seek immediate medical attention or call your healthcare provider if you experience:

Severe, sudden foot pain that limits walking.

Signs of infection: redness, swelling, heat, or drainage.

Numbness so profound that you cannot feel heat or cold.

Open sores or ulcers on your feet.

Rapid worsening of diabetic control (extreme thirst, frequent urination).

For non-emergency concerns about persistent foot pain, schedule an appointment with your primary care doctor, podiatrist, or endocrinologist. They can confirm whether your symptoms reflect Morton's neuroma vs diabetic nerve damage—and set you on the right path to relief.
