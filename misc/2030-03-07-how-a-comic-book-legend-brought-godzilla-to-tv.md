WHAT’S MATT FRACTION up to? A household name in the comic book industry thanks to his work on The Invincible Iron Man, The Immortal Iron Fist, Uncanny X-Men, Fantastic Four, Thor, Sex Criminals, Casanova, and Hawkeye, Fraction’s last published series, Superman’s Pal Jimmy Olsen, arrived in 2020. The prolific writer’s output slowed in recent years for good reason: He’s spent the last five years working on a Godzilla series for Apple TV+ alongside TV veteran Chris Black (Severance, Invincible, Star Trek: Enterprise).

The series, Monarch: Legacy of Monsters, is a time-spanning epic with quite the series of hooks: The show dives into the origins of the shadowy organization that’s existed throughout all the MonsterVerse movies, dating back to Kong: Skull Island and continuing through to Godzilla vs. Kong. After Godzilla’s re-emergence in 2014, aka G-Day, school teacher Cate (Anna Sawai) discovers that her father had a whole separate life and family in Japan. She partners with her newfound brother, Kentaro (Ren Watabe), to discover what their father was up to and how it intersects with an old Monarch staffer named Lee Shaw—played in flashback by Wyatt Russell and in the modern day by Kurt Russell.

Yes, father and son are playing the same role. It’s a series that touches on many of the themes seen in Fraction’s work, including a decidedly familial thematic approach.

Fraction spoke to Men’s Health about how the Russells came to join the show, how long he’s spent working on the series, what it meant to work on it as a fan of the franchise, working with WandaVision (and future Fantastic Four) director Matt Shakman, shaking Godzilla’s hand and much more.

Men’s Health: It’s important to go back to the beginning. How did Monarch: Legacy of Monsters first come to you?

Matt Fraction: This was on the table literally at the end of the coffee meeting when my wife and I met the folks at Legendary. As we were speculating about things—Legendary has a lot of incredible IP, and our deal was to not just develop our own material. I knew it [a Godzilla series] was a thing that they had been trying to get going and hadn't had luck with. It always struck me as nuts, if anything else, just because I would want to watch this show. It's been a five-year journey, almost to the day.

It started because I was a fan. Legendary said, "This is our toy box. What looks interesting?" I was like, "I can't believe that Godzilla show hasn't happened yet. Let's talk about that." It was like a poison chalice—it was a massive boulder that took five years to push up a hill.

How much of what is now the show was initially there between the familial elements, the prequel elements, and so forth?

The weaving of past and present was always a part of it. There's a timeline in the films, but there's such big gaps between them. So the intention was always that time would recede in the shows, and then return again.

I think it's really interesting that from the Gareth Edwards film in 2014, to Godzilla: King of the Monsters, it was 2014 and 2019. It was five years of time in the universe. The difference of Monarch in 2014—when no one knew who they were or why they should listen to them—to [2019], being the kind of Homeland Security with bunkers around the world, when you know to follow the Monarch people… that was fascinating. How did the super secret, tin foil hat, division of scientists and recluses become this public-facing, "Hi! Trust us when the big monsters come, do what we say! kind of guys?

That was a cool place to put the world. That world looks totally different, where you don't have to have people [say], "Oh, is Godzilla real?" Godzilla is absolutely real. Everybody knows that, right? Chris [Black] and I would shorthand it and [say], "It's not a 9/11 show. It's a 9/12 show." It's a show about the first time you have to take your shoes and belt off at the airport. The world's totally different now. Something's totally changed.

It's all about what's next.

In the movies, buildings get knocked down. In the show, it's the people who get up and have to figure out how to go on. Now we get to live. It's very much about aftermath. And legacy.

What do you remember about the first time you saw a Godzilla project?

Oh, man. I was fascinated by the special effects. They're all so charming. I saw them when I was really little. I had a grandfather that really loved science fiction and horror. And I just grew up sitting next to him, watching everything in the world. If there was a guy in a rubber suit or latex mask, he'd watch it.

The chaos and the scale, all the buildings getting knocked over, the creature design—I was a really artistic kid [who] always drew and always was drawn to that kind of thing. I would just get super into what they looked like. I got to touch one of the Godzilla suits at Toho. It was like, "I want to touch it. I've wanted to touch this. What does it feel like?" There was a textural thing, right? The spectacle and the scale and the fun. I think they're fun, right? The Toho films, in the era when I was growing up, were a lot of fun and bonkers.

What did the suit feel like?

Uhhhh, pudding! No, I don't want to say; I think everyone should have a chance to quietly shake hands with Godzilla in a secret location. I don't want to spoil that for anyone and put anything in your mind. But if you have the opportunity to shake Godzilla's hand, you should take it. It's pretty great.

Does he have a firm handshake? I feel like he would.

He's confident, very confident. He's great. He's a total pro. I mean, he shows up on time. He knows his lines; he hits his mark every single time—total pro.

He's been working for so long at this point.

Oh, he's a legend. When we were going around, pitching the show, at one point, I was like, "Yeah, no. We've got the biggest box office star in Asia and the tallest, darkest leading man in Hollywood. How does the show not happen?"

When you're talking about the size, the scale, and the scope of watching those original movies, how did you structure the show to ensure that those same elements that you found so engaging and engrossing as a kid were at the forefront of what the series was?

We knew we couldn't compete with the films, and had no interest in trying. The worst version of the show would be a scaled-down, made-for-TV version [of the films]. Especially now, as big as the MonsterVerse films are. They're so massive and so spectacular. But they are spectacle, right? They're events, the things you buy tickets for, and you leave your house to go see. Films are a thing we engage with as a community. You buy a seat, you sit in the theater, and it's a thing. Television is a thing that you invite into your home. It's where you live; it's where your stuff is, right? It's got to be driven by people. There's got to be character. So we always wanted to make a TV show. We didn't want to make a small version of the film. It can't compete with that. We wanted to make a show about the people that live in this world and discover they have a much greater connection to it than others. But it was always about this.

Was the generational, legacy, familial element something you latched onto? That's a throughline in your work, whether it's Kate and Clint in Hawkeye, the idea of fathers and sons in Casanova, or even Fantastic Four. When Chris brought that to you, was it something that you were immediately like, "Okay, I can wrap my arms around? This feels familiar to me?"

It hadn't really occurred to me, but I guess so. There's a recurring theme in the work I do that I like stories about people deciding to accept their inheritance or reject it. Children being the parents of adults, and all that kind of stuff. I don't know if Ireally had until you asked. But it's certainly there. It's certainly thematically appropriate. It's the kind of stuff I love. I like playing with the chronology, not just because, like, "Oh, we're playing with chronology," but because I think there's a way we can treat our audience as co-conspirators and collaborators. It's interesting. We know how the story ends. Monarch wins [laughs]. So how we get there is everything. All that matters is how we get there.

On the subject of families, the Kurt and Wyatt of it all is really, really fun. Was this like a chicken and egg situation? Can you walk me through how that came together? Because that feels special.

I think once we realized that Shaw was the character who was going to connect the two eras and started to think about it, it was kind of the first pick. Because the idea of, like, "Oh, they could play the same person." And Wyatt's at an age where he really looks like Kurt did in '85, '86.

I blew a take. Well, it wasn't a take—it was blocking—but I thought it was a take. You know, there's an episode where someone is showing old Monarch footage, like 16-millimeter footage, of a Monarch mission with Wyatt, and Kurt stands in front of the projection, and just for a second, Wyatt is projected right over Kurt. And it was perfect. I shouted out, "Holy shit!"

Not to speak for Kurt and for Wyatt—who are great and just attacked this with everything—but I think they had been offered father-son parts before, but this was interesting. They worked together to build the character out. It got to a point where we would refer to it as "Wyatt was Lee, but Kurt was Shaw." This story is about how does Lee become Shaw and how does Shaw rediscover what it was like to be Lee?

They would do lines together, [ask] "How would you do this?" They would go back and forth. They would read each other's parts. They just really worked the whole time to make this thing. As much fun as it is to write that, to bring it into the world of acting— this is just a treat, you know? I think it's certainly a stunt-y idea. But it's fun. Man, they were willing to play.

We had really active collaborators in the Russells. It's a great relationship, and it's fun to hang out with them. Then, to watch them do this stuff — there'll be moments where Wyatt would kind of do a Kurt impression. It's like, "Oh, it's Lee pretending to be Shaw, but he's not there yet. He's trying to be a tough guy." Then there'll be other moments where Kurt, for all the Kurtness, would find something beautiful, tender, and human in Cate, Kentaro, or May, and suddenly he would soften up. It's like, "Oh my God, look at that!" right?

Thank God Kurt and Wyatt understood it. It was more than a gimmick for us. [We told them] "We want you guys to build this thing together," and they were they absolutely wanted to—and went for it.

In talking about collaborators, what did Matt Shakman bring to the show, visually, that made him the right choice to helm these first two installments?

Matt Shakman and Jess Hall, our DP in the first two episodes, gave us the visual language of the show in ways that you might not even recognize. Matt's great gift as a director is his humanity, his ability to focus on humanity regardless of the craziness, whether it's Chippendales or WandaVision, no matter how big. In WandaVision, it's literally big and crazy VFX stuff, but it's always about these people. Matt can always center the human experience among all the chaos going on. He has such a gift, and was so thrilled to get to touch these toys and make his Godzilla movie. He just had idea after idea. He built the cinematic language the show lives in, and then he helped us bring the characters out through our actors, and they put in the performances. And Jess Hall is a genius.

You talked about comics being this collaborative medium, and anybody who knows anything about comics knows that’s absolutely true. What was the thing that you had to learn that was inherently different from comics to make the transition to television writing and being in a writers' room?

I was thrilled to work with actors. In comics, the more I work with a collaborator, the better I feel like I know them, the better I feel like, "Oh, I don't need to write this or don't need to write that." I feel like I get better at writing for my collaborators the more I do it. I found then, to my surprise, the same thing was happening with our actors.

Once the show was on its feet, and we were going, it was like, "Oh, I'm not just writing for this character, I know who I'm writing." I can hear it in my head now, because I know what Kiersey Clemons sounds like or whatever. That was the most revelatory, how it was like, "Wow, writing the dialogue that these actors are going to say is the closest thing to writing for an artist." It's the thing that you see; it's the thing that you'd hear, right? I guess it makes sense. It was just also the most fun, quite frankly, because also, like writing for an artist, I'm not going to out-act these amazing actors. So the more space and range you gave them to be amazing, they were amazing.
