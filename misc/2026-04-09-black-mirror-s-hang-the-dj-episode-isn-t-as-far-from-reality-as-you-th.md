# Black Mirror's "Hang the DJ" Episode Isn't as Far From Reality as You Think

Black Mirror — Charlie Brooker’s brilliant, bleak take on technology, the future, and the myriad ways your smartwatch wants to kill you — aimed its sights on modern dating apps with season 4’s “Hang The DJ.” (Warning: spoilers follow.)

The episode, which was written by Brooker, follows Amy (Georgina Campbell) and Frank (Joe Cole), in a distant future where “The System” pairs couples off in romantic relationships that have set expiration dates. Think Happn meets Logan’s Run.

In the end, it’s revealed that the story we’re seeing is one of many simulations being run inside an actual dating app, measuring how many times Amy and Frank defy The System to be together. Basically, if you elope 998 times out of a thousand and you’re a perfect match.

Compared to Black Mirror’s usual soul-crushing conclusions, “Hang The DJ” is downright chipper, which is why many fans have taken to social media to proclaim it their favorite episode of the season. But how realistic is the technology? We reached out to dating app founders to get their input on the episode. Surprisingly (or perhaps unsurprisingly, if you're a diehard Black Mirror fan), the high-tech simulation in "Hang the DJ" is closer to a reality than you'd think.

We already know that dating apps have a ton of data about their users, and that some of this data can predict compatibility in surprising ways. “We just did this study that came out a couple months ago about food and its impact on dating profiles. We found that if you include the word ‘guacamole’ in your profile you are, like, 144% more likely to get a message,” Jenn Takahashi, senior Communications Manager at online dating service Zoosk, told Men’s Health. While Takahashi admits that this is “nowhere near Black Mirror kind of data,” it does point to a possible future where ones and zeroes dictate our dating lives.

Dawoon Kang, co-founder and COO of Coffee Meets Bagel, said that while the future of dating apps won't look exactly like “Hang The DJ," "with VR and AI technology, I think we will see more [personal] involvement from dating apps and more of a seamless connection between the digital world and reality." The idea of virtual dating isn't actually that far off: just last year, Conde Naste Entertainment debuted Virtually Dating, a Facebook Watch series that sends couples on virtual reality blind dates. (The show itself, however, wasn't that successful: the New York Post ran a story on it with the headline, “Virtual Reality Dating Is Here, and It’s Terrible.”).

A few months later, Tinder announced plans to introduce artificial intelligence-based features that Greg Blatt, the CEO of Tinder parent company Match.com, promised would “blur lines between the physical and digital world." The resulting tech — announced with the oddly dystopian phrase “You can’t search for it. You can’t find it”— was fairly underwhelming, but it has echoes of the AI decision-making process of Black Mirror’s System.

That said, there are some things dating apps will probably never do. “I can tell you that in Bumble we’re probably never going to be revealing how long you’re going to be with somebody,” Alex Williamson el-Effendi, Bumble’s Head of Brand, told Men’s Health. “What we believe in, as a brand, is that you are in control of your destiny.”

There’s also limits to the technology that we currently have at our disposal. Last year, found that, in terms of predicting the success of individual couples, the algorithms powering dating apps are fairly hit-or-miss. In fact, couples that match on dating apps are about as likely to be successful as couples who met at a bar.

And that, really, is the tension of “Hang The DJ,” an episode that can be viewed as either anxiety-inducing or life-affirming depending on how comfortable you are letting data select your dates. Can “true” love start behind a computer screen? Is swiping right as effective as catching someone’s eyes from across the room? If the numbers say it works, would you put the word “guacamole” in your dating profile?

It all depends on if you believe that science can create chemistry.

“In today's society, there is a certain level of uneasiness around technology influencing something like love. That is so fundamental to we who are,” Kang said. “Online dating is often stereotyped as something that is negatively impacting the way we love. The ending of this episode makes you think, yes, technology is changing the way we love but it's not a bad thing. Love is love and it's beautiful and exciting no matter how it begins.”
