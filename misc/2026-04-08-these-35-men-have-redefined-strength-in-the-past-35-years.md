# These 35 Men Have Redefined Strength in the Past 35 Years

STRENGTH HAS ALWAYS been about muscle. But through the past three and a half decades at Men’s Health, we’ve watched as strength has also come to mean resilience, versatility, fearlessness, stamina, dedication, resolve, power, and the ability to change hearts and minds based sheerly upon who you are. You want proof? Presenting, in no particular order, 35 extraordinary men.

Lewis Hamilton

We get why you didn’t watch F1 before the Billion Dollar Man arrived in 2007: The sport seemed stuffy and niche and, despite all its mph, kind of boring. And maybe you still weren’t watching in 2008, when Hamilton won his first Formula 1 championship (the first Black racer ever to do so). But by the time he won his seventh and was all over Netflix’s Drive to Survive, you had to have marveled at his mental strength, needed to stay completely in the zone for the two hours of a race, and his physical strength, needed to withstand backbreaking speed. He’s velocity incarnate, and he’s not slowing down.

Chadwick Boseman

To watch Boseman as T’Challa in Black Panther is to behold an actor in his prime. He had a superhero’s build and an emotional power you couldn’t find anywhere else in the MCU. But then you learn that he was shooting that Oscar-winning film (and six other movies) while undergoing treatment—physically debilitating, mentally devastating—for the colorectal cancer that would eventually kill him in 2020, and that he did so without flagging. Being able to play one of Marvel’s most vaunted superheroes while living in that much pain isn’t just superheroic. It’s superhuman.

Alex Honnold

He’s climbed the 1,300-foot Moonlight Buttress in Zion National Park, the 2,500-foot El Sendero Luminoso in Mexico, and the nearly 3,000-foot El Capitan in Yosemite. And he’s done them all free solo—without a rope or safety equipment, using just his body. You’d think he’d be fierce and focused, but he comes off as humble and chill. “I understand the risks involved,” he’s said, “but [for] the hundreds of days of joy I get going out and being in the mountains, I’m willing to take those tiny chances.”

Bo Jackson

In 1989, Jackson was the MLB All-Star Game MVP. In 1990, he was selected for the NFL Pro Bowl. No athlete dominated two sports like Bo. He wasn’t only an outfielder and a running back; he completely owned the positions. Numerous injuries hampered his later career and forced him to retire, but he’s still dominating multiple sports (including cycling and golf). Bo knows well-rounded strength.

LeBron James

Thirty-eight years old. Twenty seasons in the NBA. Four championships spread among three teams. Three Olympic golds. The all-time scoring leader (sorry, Kareem). The only NBA player—ever—to notch 30,000 points, 10,000 rebounds, and 10,000 assists. But that’s just basketball. He’s powerful in all senses of the word, especially as an agent for change. He’s also the NBA’s first billionaire and invests in Black content creators through his company SpringHill while doing charitable work with Boys & Girls Clubs of America, Black Lives Matter, and the NAACP Legal Defense Fund. That’s strength, as a leader, multiplied.

The Other Chrises

Along with Hemsworth, there’s Chris Pratt and Chris Evans, who collectively became part of a meme in the mid-2010s (oh, those pre-Covid years) around the issue of who was the “best Chris.” The answer, really, is all of them. They’ve all had wildly successful careers (Thor, Star-Lord, Captain America—come on), and each has gone on to embody a different type of physical strength (Hemsworth’s mega muscle, Pratt’s buff-dad physique, Evans’s quarterback cut). They’ve been pretty chill about the whole “One has to go” thing. Because all of them, obviously, have staying power.

Arnold Schwarzenegger

At the heart of building muscle is the idea that whenever your body can handle a weight, you should challenge it with something more. Few epitomize this better than Schwarzenegger, who started his career collecting seven Mr. Olympia titles by age 33 but then began pushing himself in other areas, eventually conquering Hollywood. Later he challenged himself in politics, becoming governor of California and fighting for better climate policies. Even at age 76, his dedication is formidable. As he recently told us, he trains to “stay alive.”

RuPaul Charles

The indisputable Queen of Drag, RuPaul built an empire around becoming someone else. At a towering six-foot-four, he has cleared the runway for others he’s inspired while flexing his vision of a gender-free culture. For the 15 years of the just-renewed RuPaul’s Drag Race, he’s ended each episode with “If you can’t love yourself, how in the hell are you gonna love somebody else?” For someone who has stood up against anti-LGBTQ+ hate for decades, can we get an amen up in here?

Eugene Goodman

Of all the scenes from January 6, 2021, perhaps none was worse than that of a rioter attacking Goodman with a Confederate flag. Prior to joining the U. S. Capitol Police, Goodman served four years in the Army, including one deployment to the dangerous Sunni triangle in Iraq. His fellow service members have called him “calm, cool, and collected under fire.” Which, if you rewatch those January 6 clips, you’ll witness as Goodman steadily leads the mob away from the Senate chamber, an act of bravery that would later earn him the Congressional Gold Medal.

Steve Gleason

[in the Past Years](https://cortexcompass.jcesqc8.com/blog/3901838033.html)

The former NFL safety and special-teams player was diagnosed with ALS in 2011. With his wife, Michel, he formed Team Gleason to convey and demonstrate to those with the terminal neuromuscular disease that more living was possible. The team worked with Microsoft to build eye-tracking software, the Senate to pass legislation ensuring access to adaptive technology, and others with ALS to organize travel adventures.

Anthony Fauci, M.D.

Before Covid, there was the AIDS crisis of the 1980s, when Dr. Fauci was the director of the National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases. As with Covid, he was buffeted by criticism, this time by LGBTQ+ protesters who demanded treatment through experimental research. So he met with them and listened. HIV-AIDS, Ebola, avian flu—Dr. Fauci was there through it all—and then Covid. During the pandemic, he fell back on his resolve—in briefings, in the media, everywhere. With a calm voice and no-nonsense manner, he kept America focused.

When we talked to Dr. Fauci in 2020, his tension level was pretty high. His fix then: “I do a power walk for about three and a half to four miles, and that generally decompresses my stress.”

Michael Phelps

Eight years ago, when Phelps appeared on the cover of Sports Illustrated (again), I wasn’t surprised. He had already made a name for himself as a cold-blooded champion and a poster boy for physical strength. Yes, he talked about his training routine, his gut-busting meal plan, and breaking Mark Spitz’s record. But then Phelps discussed his drinking habit, his troubled relationship with his father, his once being in a really dark place—and eventually going to therapy. The year that article was published, I listened as several of my patients told me that it was Phelps’s story that inspired them to focus on their mental health. The most decorated Olympian in history taught us that the strongest men are those who talk, are vulnerable, and have the courage to get help when they need it. —Gregory Scott Brown, M.D., is a psychiatrist and Men’s Health advisor.

Magic Johnson

In 1991, when Magic held a press conference to announce he had HIV, he had 12 seasons and five championships as a Laker to his name. He was—and still is—the strongest point guard of all time. But on that day, TV anchors called him “a statistic.” What was then considered a death sentence Magic adopted as a mantle, declaring he would become a spokesperson in the fight against the disease and vowing to push for HIV prevention. Then came the 1992 All-Star Game and the Dream Team. Then his 32 games as a Laker again in 1995–96. Then the Basketball Hall of Fame in 2002. And now here he is, in 2023, a co-owner of the Washington Commanders, ready to make his mark on another sport, another culture, as a titan of change, and anything but “a statistic.”

Jackie Chan

The “next Bruce Lee” had been making slapstick martial-arts movies for global audiences for years before Rush Hour blew up in the U. S. in 1998. At just over five-foot-eight, Chan is relatively small but tenacious. His résumé boasts more than 140 acting roles and 80 stunt credits, and over the course of his 50-year career, his injuries have included (but are not limited to!) several broken noses, a dislocated sternum, nearly losing an eye, aaaaand a partial skull fracture that required brain surgery. Yet, somehow, he smiles through it all.

Michael J. Fox

Fox was 37 when he publicly shared that he had Parkinson’s disease—but he had already known for seven years. In those seven years he drank heavily. He self-sabotaged. But then he sobered up and tried something else: optimism. He kept acting and built what would become the Michael J. Fox Foundation, a nonprofit that has to date brought 15 Parkinson’s interventions to clinical trial. And he’s open about it all—in all his stammering and frailty, which only makes him appear stronger for speaking out.

Lionel Messi and Cristiano Ronaldo

Their rivalry for the top spot in soccer has defined the sport for two decades. Ronaldo, the six-foot-two Portuguese striker, is a goal-scoring phenom. Messi, the five-foot-seven Argentine forward, is a complete playmaker. Ronaldo has 200 abs. Messi is squat and burly. When Messi captained his country to a World Cup win in 2022, it cemented his status as the GOAT. He now plays in Miami, and Ronaldo in Saudi Arabia, but they’re still inspiring each other and us. Here’s to strength in rivalry.

Eddie Hall

Precious few humans can match the raw strength numbers of Hall, who became the first person ever to deadlift 500 kilograms (that’s 1,102 pounds) back in 2016. But this strongman is more than numbers. Seven years removed from that record, Hall continues to explore the capabilities of his massive 362-pound frame. Last year, that meant entering the boxing ring (against fellow strongman Hafthór Björnsson) and testing himself in the NFL’s most fabled benchmark, the 40-yard dash. And sure, he lost the boxing match and ran a sundial-slow 5.73 seconds (albeit in full pads!), but he still dared to leave his comfort zone to push his body.

Understand this about Dwayne Johnson: When you see him punch out the bad guys on the big screen or tackle Bulgarian split squats on Instagram, you’re witnessing the final product of decades of work in the gym. Pumping iron has always come easy to Johnson, once a heralded University of Miami football recruit. Jason Collins

[Strength in the Past](https://lumina-dermis.sunsimiaosd.com/blog/9571988545.html)

In April of 2013, about two years before same-sex marriage would become legal in the U. S., Collins wrote a personal essay in Sports Illustrated that started with “I’m a 34-year-old NBA center. I’m black. And I’m gay.” He was the first pro athlete in any of the four major American sports leagues to go public while an active player. Gay men, of course, had been playing in professional sports for decades; some of them would come out in retirement and some would never come out at all, fearing, perhaps, the possibility of losing so much. But Collins, knowing the risks (real and imagined), told the truth anyway. His courage cleared the field for the likes of the NFL’s Carl Nassib and the NHL’s Luke Prokop, both in 2021. And Collins keeps fighting. “What’s left,” he said recently, “is to get to the point where the coming out of a male athlete is no different than their female counterparts.”
