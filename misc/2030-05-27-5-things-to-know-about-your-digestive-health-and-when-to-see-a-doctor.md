# 5 Things to Know About Your Digestive Health, and When to See a Doctor

You probably don’t worry much about your digestive health unless there’s something seriously wrong. Even then? Gnarly stomachaches, gas, and not-so-normal bowel movements aren’t exactly fun or easy to talk about, so stomach troubles often get ignored for too long. But your gut health is essential to your overall health. And when it’s out of whack, your body may not be able to absorb key nutrients, and your energy, immune function, weight, and even mood could suffer as a result.

That’s why knowing the basics of digestive health—and how to keep yours in check—is key. Here, five of the most important things to know about taking care of your pipes, plus when it’s time for a gut check.

1. It’s normal to go three times a day or three times a week.

Staying regular is the cornerstone of good gut health, but how often you go number two will vary from person to person, says Diana A. Whitehead, M.D., a gastroenterologist and director of the Inflammatory Bowel Disease Center at Greater Baltimore Medical Center.

Usually, pooping anywhere from three times a day to three times a week is normal. But anything outside of that is cause for concern, says Dr. Whitehead. Talk to your doctor if you notice you’re suddenly constipated or having diarrhea. Shifts like this could be a sign of short-term problems (a stomach bug or a need for more fiber) or something more serious like an off-kilter thyroid or a digestive disorder like ulcerative colitis (which can typically be treated with medication and tweaks to your diet).

2. Don’t ignore heartburn that strikes twice a week.

Experiencing heartburn after inhaling a plate of loaded chili cheese fries make perfect sense, but heartburn that strikes more than twice a week or doesn’t go away with OTC antacids means it’s time to call your doctor, says Dr. Whitehead.

This could be a sign of gastroesophageal reflux disease (a.k.a. GERD) or acid reflux, which happens when stomach acid backs up into your esophagus (the mouth-to-stomach tube). The good news? Heartburn is typically easy to treat with prescription meds and lifestyle changes such as weight loss or quitting smoking.

3. Colorectal cancer is common and colonoscopies are the most accurate and effective way to check for it.

“Cancer prevention is an essential piece of digestive health,” says Navdeep Chehl, M.D., a board-certified gastroenterologist with Austin Gastroenterology in Austin, TX. Screening for colorectal cancer has been proven to save lives—one recent study found that a colonoscopy can reduce the risk of death from colorectal cancer by 61 percent. Yet many guys don’t make time for for a variety of reasons: they’re busy, work calls, it’s out of budget, or the procedure itself just sounds kind of scary. What they should be more afraid of is dying young.

Most guys don’t need to start screening until they’re 45 (according to the American Cancer Society) or 50 (according to the National Cancer Institute), but there are a number of factors that could put you at an increased risk, including family history, obesity, a smoking habit, or even race (African American men are at an increased risk). “Talk to your doctor about when you should start screenings and how often to check in,” suggests Dr. Chehl.

4. Fiber, probiotics, and prebiotics fuel good gut health.

“Fiber helps move things along in the digestive tract, keeping your gut regular and healthy,” says Amanda A. Kostro Miller, RD, LDN, who serves on the advisory board for Fitter Living. Guys should aim for 38 grams a day. “Foods that are high in fiber include bran cereals with whole grain products, fruits, vegetables, beans, peas and other legumes, nuts, and seeds,” says Dr. Whitehead.

Probiotics are live, microscopic organisms that help your gut function normally. They can be found in Kefir, yogurt, soy products, Kombucha and many fermented foods, including kimchi, sauerkraut, pickled foods and tempeh. Prebiotics are a type of fiber that probiotic bacteria consume, and are found in garlic, onion, asparagus and other foods. Consuming probiotic and prebiotic foods will help grow helpful gut bacteria that we need for a healthy immune system, Kostro Miller says. “Research shows that probiotics improve gut health, and good gut health is linked to improved immunity.”

Having a diversity of healthy gut bacteria helps protect the body from chronic health conditions and autoimmune diseases. A healthy gut microbiome can significantly reduce your risk of developing depression, type 1 diabetes, obesity, even cancer.

5. Your mind and your gut are connected.

Researchers have found that your mind speaks to your gut and vice versa, explains Dr. Whitehead. “Gastrointestinal problems can create anxiety and stress. In addition, stress and anxiety can make gastrointestinal symptoms worse.” (Ever feel “butterflies in your stomach” before a big presentation?) The takeaway: Sinking your stress levels is key to maintaining good gut health. If you’re noticing stomach woes pop up during tumultuous times, keep a symptom diary of when your issues strike and what’s going on in your life that might be contributing. Notice a link? Seeing a therapist could be helpful, says Dr. Whitehead.
