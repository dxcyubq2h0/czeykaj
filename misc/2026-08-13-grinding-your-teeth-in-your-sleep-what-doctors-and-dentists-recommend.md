Explanation

Teeth grinding at night (bruxism) affects up to 30% of adults and can silently damage your teeth, jaw, and overall well-being. While occasional grinding isn't usually dangerous, chronic bruxism can lead to headaches, tooth fractures, jaw pain and disrupted sleep for you and your partner. Below, expert recommendations from both medical and dental professionals will help you understand causes, spot warning signs and explore effective treatments.

What Is Teeth Grinding at Night (Bruxism)?

Bruxism is the habit of clenching or grinding your teeth unconsciously, especially during sleep. It falls into two categories:

Sleep bruxism: Involuntary grinding during sleep, often linked to sleep disorders.

Awake bruxism: Daytime clenching or grinding, frequently tied to stress or concentration.

Common Signs and Symptoms

You might not notice you grind your teeth until damage becomes obvious. Key clues include:

Worn, flattened or chipped teeth

Tooth sensitivity to hot, cold or pressure

Sore jaw muscles, especially upon waking

Frequent headaches around the temples

Ear pain or ringing (tinnitus)

Popping or clicking in the jaw (TMJ joint)

Disrupted sleep for you or your partner

If you tick off several of these symptoms, it's time to take action.

Potential Causes of Bruxism

While exact causes vary, doctors and dentists agree on several common triggers:

Stress and anxiety: Emotional tension often spills over into jaw clenching.

Sleep disorders: Conditions like sleep apnea can worsen nighttime grinding.

Abnormal bite or missing/crooked teeth: Misalignment forces extra pressure when you chew or clench.

Lifestyle factors: Excessive caffeine, alcohol or smoking can aggravate bruxism.

Diagnosis: How Doctors and Dentists Identify Bruxism

Clinical examination: Your dentist inspects teeth for wear patterns and asks about jaw pain or headaches.

Patient history: A doctor will explore your stress levels, sleep quality and medication use.

Bite analysis: Models or digital scans can reveal misalignments requiring correction.

Sleep study (polysomnography): In complex cases or suspected sleep apnea, an overnight test may be recommended.

Questionnaires and diaries: Tracking daily clenching episodes or stressors helps pinpoint triggers.

Accurate diagnosis guides personalized treatment and prevents unnecessary interventions.

Management and Treatment Options

1. Custom Mouth Guards and Splints

Occlusal splints: Soft or hard plastic devices fitted over your upper or lower teeth to cushion and distribute pressure.

Night guards: Prevent direct tooth-to-tooth contact, reducing wear and easing jaw muscle tension.

Protect enamel

Minimize headaches and jaw pain

Easy to adapt to, when custom-made by your dentist

2. Stress Management Techniques

Relaxation exercises: Deep breathing, progressive muscle relaxation or guided imagery before bed.

Mindfulness and meditation: Daily practice reduces overall muscle tension.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): Helps reframe negative thought patterns fueling daytime clenching.

3. Behavioral and Lifestyle Modifications

Limit stimulants: Cut back on caffeine, especially after lunch.

Reduce alcohol and tobacco: Both substances increase muscle activity during sleep.

Rest your tongue between teeth during the day

Gently stretch jaw muscles

Avoid chewing non-food items: Chewing gum, pens or ice can train your jaw to clench more.

4. Dental Corrections

Orthodontics: Braces or aligners can fix bite misalignment contributing to bruxism.

Restorative work: Crowns or bridges restore worn teeth and rebalance your bite.

Equilibration (occlusal adjustment): Minor reshaping of tooth surfaces to improve how your teeth meet.

5. Physical Therapy and Biofeedback

Jaw exercises and massage: A physical therapist can teach techniques to release tension in the masseter and temporalis muscles.

Biofeedback devices: Sensors alert you when clenching occurs, helping you learn to relax your jaw muscles.

6. Medication (Selective Use)

Muscle relaxants: Short-term prescriptions may ease severe jaw tightness.

Botulinum toxin (Botox): In refractory cases, small injections into jaw muscles can reduce grinding for several months.

Caution: Medication isn't a stand-alone cure and carries side-effect risks—discuss thoroughly with your doctor.

Create a relaxing bedtime routine (warm bath, reading, gentle stretching)

Limit screen time 1–2 hours before bed
