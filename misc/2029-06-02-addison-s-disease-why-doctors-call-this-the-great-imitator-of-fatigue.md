Persistent fatigue paired with unexplained weight loss, low blood pressure, salt cravings, and skin darkening (hyperpigmentation) often signals Addison's disease — a rare adrenal insufficiency disorder known as "the great imitator" of chronic fatigue.

Addison's disease develops when the adrenal glands fail to produce sufficient cortisol and aldosterone. Diagnosis generally includes hormone testing (ACTH stimulation and cortisol levels), adrenal imaging (CT or MRI), and autoimmune antibody screening. Treatment involves lifelong hormone replacement therapy, emergency cortisol injections during adrenal crises, and lifestyle adjustments such as stress management and increased salt intake during illness or hot weather.

Explanation

Addison's disease, also known as primary adrenal insufficiency, occurs when the adrenal glands don't produce enough cortisol and often not enough aldosterone. Because its symptoms can mimic so many other common conditions, it has earned the nickname "the great imitator" of fatigue. Understanding why fatigue in Addison's disease often goes unrecognized—and what to look for—can help you and your doctor reach the right diagnosis more quickly.

How Healthy Adrenal Glands Work

Your adrenal glands sit on top of each kidney and make several essential hormones:

Cortisol• Supports energy production by regulating blood sugar• Helps your body cope with stress• Controls inflammation

Aldosterone• Manages sodium and potassium balance• Keeps blood pressure stable

Androgens (small amounts)• Influence muscle mass, mood, and libido

When these hormones drop too low, especially cortisol, your entire body can feel out of balance.

Why Fatigue Is Just the Tip of the Iceberg

Fatigue is the most common symptom of Addison's disease—but it's far from the only one. Because so many health issues cause tiredness, Addison's disease can be mistaken for:

Depression or Anxiety

Hypothyroidism

Chronic Fatigue Syndrome

Common Signs and Symptoms

Beyond persistent fatigue, look for these clues:

• Unexplained Weight Loss You may lose several pounds without changing your diet or exercise habits.

• Muscle Weakness and Joint Pain Everyday activities feel more difficult.

• Gastrointestinal Upset Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, or diarrhea may come and go.

• Dizziness or Low Blood Pressure Especially when standing up quickly (orthostatic hypotension).

• Salt Cravings A sudden preference for salty foods may signal low aldosterone.

• Darkening of the Skin (Hyperpigmentation) Often most noticeable in scars, skin folds, and gums.

• Mood Changes Irritability, depression, or "brain fog."

• Low Blood Sugar (Hypoglycemia) More common in children and people with severe cortisol deficiency.

Why Diagnosis Is Challenging

Fatigue and muscle weakness are so common that Addison's often isn't on the radar.

Anemia, thyroid disorders, and mental health issues all cause similar complaints.

Sometimes symptoms develop over months or years, making patterns hard to spot.

Primary adrenal insufficiency affects about 1 in 100,000 people annually.

Tests and Evaluation

If your doctor suspects Addison's disease, they may order:

Blood Tests

Morning cortisol and ACTH levels

Sodium, potassium, and glucose levels

ACTH Stimulation Test

Measures cortisol response after a synthetic ACTH injection

A poor response confirms adrenal insufficiency

Autoantibody Screening

Looks for antibodies that attack the adrenal glands (most common in autoimmune Addison's)

Imaging

CT scan of the adrenal glands to check for damage or enlargement

Additional Hormone Tests

Evaluate thyroid and other pituitary hormones to rule out secondary causes

Lifestyle Adjustments

Maintain a balanced diet with adequate salt and fluids.

Carry medical alert identification.

Inform family, friends, and coworkers about your emergency plan.

Get regular check-ups to adjust medication doses as needed.

When to Be Alert for Adrenal Crisis

An adrenal crisis is a life-threatening emergency marked by severe cortisol deficiency. Symptoms include:

Sudden, severe pain (abdomen, legs, or lower back)

Profound weakness or confusion

High fever, vomiting, or diarrhea

Low blood pressure leading to fainting or shock

If you or a loved one experiences these signs, seek emergency medical care immediately.
