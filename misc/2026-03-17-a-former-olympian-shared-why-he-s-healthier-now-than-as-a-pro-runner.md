# A Former Olympian Shared Why He's Healthier Now Than as a Pro Runner

Since retiring as a professional runner, Olympic sprinter Nick Symmonds has been on a physical transformation journey, packing muscle onto the traditionally wiry runner's frame and taking on all kinds of fitness challenges in his new career as a YouTuber. In a recent video, Symmonds looked back on his days as a pro runner, and explained that while he is proud and grateful for the things he was able to achieve, he feels happier and healthier now, at 36, than he did at 28.

"In 2012, I was one of the fastest 800m runners on Planet Earth; that year I finished fifth at the Olympic Games. But there's nothing particularly healthy about doing something to that extent," says Symmonds. "The amount of damage it does to your body to be so singularly focused. I was always dealing with some little issue, whether it was a stress fracture here, or a torn ligament there, or the illnesses. I was constantly getting sick. I got a cold or flu every other month because I was training so hard and traveling so much my immune system was always run down."

Another aspect of his pro running career which he doesn't miss is the cutting ahead of a race. "Target race weight is always an unnatural weight," he says. "I lived and trained 5 to 10 pounds heavier than I actually raced. When you're cutting weight, when you're losing that much body fat, it's unnatural, and I think at the Games I was certainly in that unnatural area, and I could only hold that for a few weeks during championship season."

He goes on to say that while he was very fit according to the specific criteria of his sport, he also had very low flexibility and strength. "I was a one trick pony, and that trick was running for 1:42," he says, adding that he feels like a more well-rounded athlete these days.

And it's not just physical health: Symmonds recalls the psychological and emotional toll that this intense level of training took on him, and how his mental health suffered as a result. "I'm not sure I've ever met a professional runner who hasn't gone through some form of depression," he says. "In my 12 years as a professional runner, I spent many of those years very, very depressed."

He adds that the constant travel, being away from friends and family, and the general pressure he felt to succeed, all compounded this issue, not to mention that the highs that Olympians feel during the Games, being at the pinnacle of their careers with everybody cheering them on, inevitably leads to a crashing low once that event is over.

"Perhaps the worst is, when you do make those sacrifices and you make that investment, and then something goes wrong," he says. "Maybe you get tripped in a race, you get a stress fracture, something horrible happens and you can't capitalize on all that hard work you've put in, that's what tends to lead to spiraling depression."

"I'm healthier mentally and physically today than I ever was as a pro runner, and that leads to me being considerably happier as well," he says. "I'm so grateful that I got to run professionally for 12 years, but I'm also so grateful that it's over and I get to enjoy all the wonderful things that life has to offer. I get to spend time with my family and friends, I get to pursue other interests outside of just running in circles. I'm a more well-rounded person."
