# These Bartender Boxers Sling Booze by Day and Throw Punches at Night

Midway through his fight, a piercing pain shot up Devin Kennedy’s arm. For the past three months, he had been training for this day — sacrificing sleep, blood, and sweat — to duke it out in a final boxing bout in front of a crowd of hundreds at STAGE 48 in New York City. That night, however, Kennedy had no qualms about calling quits in the second round.

Kennedy isn’t a professional boxer whose livelihood depended on winning this fight; rather, he’s the cocktail director at the Michelin-starred restaurant Cote who had entrenched himself in a 12-week boxing program. That evening, he was more concerned about leaving the ring in good enough shape to hold a shaker the next day than winning the championship belt.

“I didn’t do it for the win,” Kennedy said about his decision to join the program, nearly a month after the fight. “I was doing it for the experience.”

Like boxing, bartending is not for the faint of heart. In addition to the stress of serving multiple drinks at once while babysitting sloppy customers, many in the hospitality industry blow off steam with drinks after hours. Their diets are usually unbalanced, as are their sleep schedules. Even bartenders themselves are usually the first to admit they are not paragons of wellness.

That's why, in 2017, bartenders in cities nationwide, including New York and Chicago, participated in the Bartender Boxing Association, a three-month training program sponsored by Tequila Cazadores aiming to transform bartenders into amateur boxing and wellness acolytes.

The idea for Bartending Boxing came from Raj Nagra, a veteran in the liquor industry for over 25 years. Nagra said boxing became a necessity to offset his industry’s taxing lifestyle, including hectic traveling and late nights spent behind the stick. He thought a boot camp might rewire hospitality employees and convince them of the value of dedicating time outside of work to fitness.

“When you're not training, and you don’t have a motivation or a real reason, you can talk or think about it, but it’s hard to act,” Nagra said. “With the boxing, the fact they’re involved in something so demanding, it reshapes the way they’re thinking on a daily basis, because they need to be able to show up at the gym and train.”

Like many who signed up for Bartending Boxing, Kennedy found the culture surrounding bartending was affecting his health, a criticism the industry has increasingly faced. (Studies have consistently reported connections between bartending and substance abuse, and researchers have found that the hospitality industry’s irregular schedule and work environments can cause significant amounts of stress.)

A few times a week after closing a shift at work, Kennedy said he might join coworkers at the bar for a couple of beers and shots, and ending late night outings with a cheese pizza or chopped cheese sandwiches.

“You’re just up late, you’re not sleeping, you’re working on minimum sleep," Kennedy said. “That stuff weighs down on you.”

After signing up for BBO in June, Kennedy joined a cohort of 11 other New York bartenders in August, who would spend the next three months training at Gleason’s Gym in Brooklyn for amateur-level sanctioned bouts against their Chicago counterparts in November. Inside the red-and-gray gym, adorned with yellowed memorabilia on the walls, their trainers, Delen “Blimp” Parsley and Leon “Cat” Taylor, drilled into the cohort the necessity of conditioning. In addition to running three miles and performing slews of pushups and crunches everyday outside the gym, inside, they performed several circuits of medicine ball slams, using ab rollers, and jumping rope.

Although Kennedy said he felt like throwing up after the first day, by the second and third months, they were adding extra rounds of circuit training.

“By end of it, it was like ‘Wow, this was pretty easy,’” Kennedy said, noting that only after the first month were they finally allowed to spar. “Our bodies were conditioned to operate at a high level for a long period of time, and that’s pretty what boxing is — being able to exude 100 percent output in three minutes.”

Beyond just exercising, many of the bartenders themselves changing their diets. In addition to basic adjustments, like drinking around 16 cups of water daily as well as cooking his own meals at home following the rule of three (protein, carbs, and vegetables), Kennedy abstained from alcohol for the first two months after discovering that he couldn't do hill runs with a throbbing hangover. This had longer-term impacts on Kennedy, who said that nowadays he “no longer drinks to get drunk.”

Others took more drastic measures: Andy Gould, the owner of the bar Scofflaw in Chicago, who trained with the Windy City cohort at Oakley Fight Club in Chicago, became a vegan, relying heavily on a diet of properly spiced rice and bean dishes as well as home-cooked vegetable dishes. In addition to his remarkable weight loss (from 190 to 158 lbs today), Gould said that with veganism, his body feels completely rejuvenated.

“When I wake up, my hands aren’t aching, my shoulders aren’t aching,” Gould said. “It’s crazy because I’m doing so much more with my hands, my shoulders, my legs.”

Since completing the program, Gould has continued his five-day-a-week workout routins, and now has his sights set on triathlete competition. Meanwhile, Kennedy, who runs at least three miles a day in addition to working out nearly five days a week, said that he wants to eventually compete in amateur boxing circuits again. Boxing, it turns out, had a impact on his outlook on life’s challenges, too.

“I’m more relaxed at work,” Kennedy said. “Getting punched in the face is a lot more hectic than your problems in the bar world.”
