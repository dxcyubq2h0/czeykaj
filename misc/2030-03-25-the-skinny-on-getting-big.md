# The Skinny on Getting Big

Trying to build 22-inch arms without steroids is like Jeff Gordon trying to win the Daytona 500 behind the wheel of your Honda. And yet every day, in every gym in America, drug-free lifters are following routines they found in bodybuilding magazines, expecting to produce muscles like those of professional bodybuilders. Even guys who know they can't get truly huge without juice get sucked in and try to maximize their natural potential by doing the routines of the "champions."

My mission here is to set the record straight. I want to explain why guys who train drug-free have to use different tactics, and show you how to use those tactics to get the best possible results. You won't come out looking like Mr. Olympia, but you'll still get closer to that look than you are now.

Without steroids, we have limited time and energy to train, so we have to focus on the most efficient muscle-building exercises. As a general rule, the more muscle groups an exercise uses, the more efficient it will be at changing the way you look. So if you want a more impressive upper body, you're better off doing dips--which work the triceps, shoulders, chest, and lats--than you are doing triceps pressdowns, which only work the triceps.

Your workout: The squat may be the best of the best exercises, since it hits all your lower-body muscles, which are the biggest on your body. Certainly, the bench press (on a slight incline, if you have any shoulder issues) is the ideal exercise for your chest, and the pullup or chinup is best for your lats.

Others: deadlift, dip, any type of row, and shoulder press. If you're newer to the gym, or have injuries that require you to do less aggressive exercises, the leg press and lat pulldown are still good moves.

Their workout: Bodybuilding magazines are full of guys doing curls, lateral raises, and all kinds of single-joint exercises for the muscles you like to look at in the mirror. But those muscles, working in isolation, won't really impact how you look to others. That's why the best strategy for the nonjuicer is to start each workout with big-muscle, multijoint exercises that hit the bigger muscle groups, and then use curls and extensions at the end of a workout, if you use them at all.

If we could make a general, blanket statement, we'd say that virtually every serious musclehead has made the mistake of doing too much. Too many exercises, too many sets, too many workouts a week. Result: injury, illness, burnout, or just plain old disappointing results.

The solution is almost always the same: Do less--fewer workouts per week, fewer exercises, fewer sets of each exercise. That's when we discover that if we turn down the volume, we can increase our focus and effort, and inevitably get better results.

Your workout: A good workout rarely includes more than 24 total sets. Many experienced lifters find they can get the job done with just a dozen sets, including warmups.

Their workout: We flipped through a typical bodybuilding magazine recently and saw:

• A back workout with three sets each of 4 exercises;

• A shoulder-and-arm workout that included 15 sets each for shoulders, biceps, and triceps (a total of 45 sets ... and that was just one of two daily workouts for this particular bodybuilder);

• A chest workout with three sets each of three different exercises. (The entire magazine, which is far from the most extreme of the bunch, had no useful and effective workouts for nonjuicers.)

Years of reading bodybuilding magazines has convinced many lifters that they need to work out 5 or even 6 days a week. But if you're lifting more than three or four times a week, you're probably making one of two mistakes: If you push yourself hard each workout, you're probably not giving your muscles and nervous system enough time to recover between workouts. Or, if you aren't pushing yourself, you're spreading your energy too thin, and not getting the type of gains you'd see if you worked harder during fewer workouts.

Your workout: For nonjuicers, a good goal is two or three total-body workouts a week. If you lack sufficient time or energy to complete a whole body workout in one session, you probably want to lift four times, dividing up your muscles so you can work all of them at least twice per week. So you might do all your lower-body exercises on Monday and Thursday, and your upper-body exercises on Tuesday and Friday.

Another popular system involves doing your "pushing" exercises (presses, squats) one workout, and your "pulling" exercises (rows, deadlifts) the next.

Two rules you don't want to break:

• Work each muscle group effectively every 48 to 72 hours. More often might lead to overtraining and diminished returns; less often and you risk not getting the results you want.

• Pace yourself. It's okay to end a workout before you have to be carried out on a stretcher. Be judicious about techniques like forced reps--when you work to the point of complete exhaustion on a set, and then a partner helps you complete several more repetitions. It seems like a good gut check, but it also drains your nervous system and zaps your strength for several days. If you try to train again before you've fully recovered, you'll get weaker instead of stronger.

Their workout: We noted above that steroids help a body grow more with less effort and recover faster between workouts, which is why juicers can get away with 10 workouts a week--two workouts a day for 5 days, with 2 days off.
