Diminished Ovarian Reserve: What Your AMH and Antral Follicle Count Tell Fertility Specialists

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) is a condition in which the ovaries contain fewer eggs than expected for a woman's age, often impacting fertility. Doctors diagnose DOR using two key tests:

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) blood test – measures hormone levels linked to remaining egg supply.

Antral follicle count (AFC) – a transvaginal ultrasound that counts visible follicles.

These results help predict how the ovaries may respond to fertility treatments such as IVF or egg donation.

Common causes and risk factors of DOR include:

Age (especially over 35)

Genetics and family history

Prior ovarian surgery

Autoimmune conditions

Smoking

Chemotherapy or radiation exposure

Egg quality—not just quantity—also plays a critical role in conception and pregnancy outcomes.

Explanation

Diminished ovarian reserve (DOR) refers to a lower quantity and quality of eggs in the ovaries than expected for a woman's age. It does not always mean you cannot conceive, but it does influence fertility treatment options and timing. Two key tests fertility specialists use to assess ovarian reserve are the Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) blood test and the Antral Follicle Count (AFC) via ultrasound. Understanding what these tests measure—and their limitations—can help you make informed decisions and discuss next steps confidently with your doctor.

What Is Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH)?

AMH is a hormone produced by the granulosa cells of early-stage (pre-antral and small antral) follicles in the ovary. It reflects the size of the pool of small follicles—and indirectly, the quantity of eggs—remaining in the ovaries.

• How the AMH test works

A simple blood draw can measure AMH levels on any day of your menstrual cycle.

Results are reported in nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL) or picomoles per liter (pmol/L).

• Interpreting AMH levels

High AMH (>5.0 ng/mL or >35 pmol/L): Often seen in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS).

Normal AMH (1.0–4.0 ng/mL or 7–29 pmol/L): Typical for women under age 35–37.

Low AMH (<1.0 ng/mL or <7 pmol/L): Suggests diminished ovarian reserve.

• Limitations of the AMH test

AMH does not predict egg quality, pregnancy chances, or menopause timing precisely.

Values can vary slightly by lab; using the same lab for repeat testing reduces variability.

AMH alone does not diagnose infertility.

What Is Antral Follicle Count (AFC)?

Antral follicles are small, fluid-filled sacs in the ovaries that contain immature eggs. A transvaginal ultrasound examines each ovary to count the visible antral follicles (usually those measuring 2–10 mm in diameter).

• How the AFC test works

Typically performed on cycle day 2, 3, or sometimes 4 under ultrasound guidance.

The sonographer counts all antral follicles on both ovaries.

• Interpreting AFC results

Good ovarian reserve: 15–30 total antral follicles.

Moderate reserve: 10–14 follicles.

Diminished reserve: fewer than 10 follicles.

Very low reserve (<5 follicles): correlates with lower response to ovarian stimulation.

• Limitations of AFC

Operator-dependent: counts can vary by technician experience and equipment quality.

Can be influenced by hormonal medications or temporary ovarian cysts.

Like AMH, AFC does not directly measure egg quality.

Why AMH and AFC Matter Together

Neither AMH nor AFC alone gives a complete picture. Together, they offer complementary insights:

• Assessing response to fertility treatment

Low AMH + low AFC: Higher risk of poor response to ovarian stimulation in IVF.

Normal AMH + high AFC: Possible PCOS pattern; may need low stimulation doses to avoid ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS).

• Personalizing your treatment plan

Fertility specialists use both tests to tailor IVF protocols, hormone dosages, and medication timing.

These measures help estimate how many eggs might be retrieved to balance safety and effectiveness.

• Predicting time to menopause (with caution)

Lower AMH/AFC values generally correlate with nearer menopause, but individual variation is wide.

Specialists may use models combining age, AMH, AFC, and other factors to give a rough estimate.

Other Factors Influencing Ovarian Reserve and Fertility

While AMH and AFC focus on egg quantity, these aspects also play a role:

• Age

Egg quality declines with age, especially after 35.

Even with a normal ovarian reserve, older eggs may have higher rates of chromosomal abnormalities.

• Lifestyle

Smoking accelerates loss of ovarian reserve.

Severe stress, extreme exercise, or eating disorders can disrupt ovarian function.

• Medical history

Prior ovarian surgery (e.g., for endometriosis) may reduce ovarian tissue.

Chemotherapy or radiation therapy can damage follicles irreversibly.

• Genetic factors

Family history of early menopause or known genetic markers may elevate risk for diminished reserve.

What to Do If You Have Diminished Ovarian Reserve

Learning you have DOR can feel overwhelming, but there are proactive steps:

• Seek fertility counseling early

Discuss results with a reproductive endocrinologist or fertility specialist.

Ask about your chances with timed intercourse, IUI (intrauterine insemination), or IVF.

• Explore fertility preservation

Egg or embryo freezing can preserve fertility potential for future use.

Ideal if you're delaying childbearing or planning treatments that may worsen reserve.

• Review ovarian stimulation options

Tailored IVF protocols, such as antagonist cycles or minimal stimulation, may optimize your response.

Some clinics offer "DOR-specific" protocols combining high-dose gonadotropins with adjuvants.

When to Speak to a Doctor Immediately

While diminished ovarian reserve itself is not life-threatening, some related conditions merit prompt medical attention:

• Sudden pelvic pain or heavy bleeding• Signs of ovarian hyperstimulation (severe abdominal pain, shortness of breath) during fertility treatment• Symptoms of POI such as hot flashes, irregular periods, or night sweats in your 20s or 30s

If you experience any of these, contact your healthcare provider right away or visit the nearest emergency department.

Final Thoughts

Diminished ovarian reserve is a common finding that influences—but does not end—your fertility journey. AMH and AFC tests provide valuable data on your egg quantity, helping fertility specialists shape a personalized plan. Remember:

DOR does not measure egg quality or guarantee infertility.

Early evaluation allows more options, including fertility preservation.

Lifestyle habits and overall health also impact fertility success.

Emotional support and clear communication with your fertility team are vital.

For personalized guidance, speak with your doctor or a reproductive endocrinologist about your AMH, AFC, and next steps. Always seek medical advice for anything that could be serious or life-threatening.
