# Men Over 40 Should Try This Deceptively Tough Lunge Variation

Trainer, author, and fitness model Kirk Charles, NASM-CPT CES, knows that as you get older, life can get more complicated. But that shouldn’t prevent you from being on top of your game. He’ll help to answer the tough training questions that come with age so you too can be Fit Beyond 40.

Maintaining lower body strength is essential for older men, especially if you play sports that require bursts of power. As we age, we must continue to exercise the hips and glutes in targeted ways so we can move freely and help prevent injury. One exercise that can help to keep us ready for action is the curtsy lunge. The movement tests your strength, balance, and rotational mobility. If you’ve been suffering with inactivity and lack of lower body strength, these lunges—which are anything but dainty—will help you get back on track.

To start, stand in an athletic stance with your feet about six inches apart. Both feet should be facing forward. Step back with one leg—but unlike a reverse lunge, where your foot comes straight back as it it were on a train track, break the sagittal plane (purely front-to-back movement) and cross over behind the opposite foot. Descend into ta lunge, bringing your rear knee as close to the ground as possible. Keep your hips and shoulders as square (facing forward) as possible. After a quick beat, come back up to your starting position.

The curtsy lunge is a great move because you will work your glutes, specifically the glute medius muscle, which helps to stabilize your hips during movement. It’s a muscle that can be overlooked on days where you’re working your legs using other movements. Also, you’re working on rotational power and mobility, so your core is more engaged than with a regular reverse lunge.

The curtsy lunge does present a few challenges not present in other variations of the exercise. First is maintaining balance when bringing your lunge leg back and crossing it over the track of your other foot. In my younger days, this was an easy move because I had more rotational mobility. Now I cannot cross my back leg over the track as far and easily as I used to. That said, don’t worry about crossing over as much as some of your younger friends at the gym.

While descending into the curtsy lunge, the shin of your lead leg will most likely tilt to the side a bit due to rotation and maintaining your balance. Start by holding on to an anchor point to help with your balance. If you struggle to progress, consider just performing the movement with this assistance. Ankle and foot mobility and stability might also be challenging. If you’re not used to the curtsy lunge, balance may be an issue that could put extra pressure on both ankles. Again, work to progress up to the movement.

If you’re able to do it, the curtsy lunge will be a great move to add to your arsenal. You can challenge yourself even more by holding dumbbells at your side or front racking kettlebells. However, start with bodyweight to nail the balance first by doing three sets of 8 to 10 repetitions per leg.
