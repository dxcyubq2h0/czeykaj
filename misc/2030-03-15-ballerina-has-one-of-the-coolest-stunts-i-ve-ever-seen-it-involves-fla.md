THIS IS A story about From the World of John Wick: Ballerina (or, as we'll refer to it from here on out, just Ballerina). But to properly explain the magic of something very specific to Ballerina, I'm going to need to first talk about a different movie: 28 Weeks Later.

With the release later this month of 28 Years Later, the second sequel to the cult favorite Danny Boyle-directed survival horror 28 Days Later, I've been finally catching up on things. Not long ago, I watched 28 Days Later for the very first time, and it was brilliant. I almost liken it, in some ways, to the original Alien—it's quiet at times, claustrophobic and full of dread before exploding at the end in a fit of action, violence, and horror.

And with a military operation, given that this is a movie, comes something else: fire. This is the part where I reveal that on the same day I watched 28 Weeks Later, I also watched Ballerina. There's not a ton in common between these two movies; one is about an outbreak of infected, zombie-esque rage sickos, and the other is about a trained killer on a hunt for revenge. But there is one thing that connects the two, and that's that both feature lots and lots of that fire we talked about earlier.

28 Weeks Later came out in 2007, making it now nearly 20 years old. And while effects have largely come a long way, there are some moments where the fire on screen doesn't look like the fire in the real world. but rather like effects in a video game or an even older movie. Obviously none of this stuff is happening, but dated effects can have that impact on a viewing experience (28 Years Later, still rules, though, to be clear). In Ballerina, this is is never the case—and particularly not during one extended sequence near the end of the movie that does something I've never seen before.

In Ballerina there is a flamethrower fight. Yes, that's right: a full-on flamethrower fight.

Part of that, of course, is because there were hardly any effects involved at all. Yet again, Ballerina is making the case for a movie to use as many practical effects as possible. CGI sometimes ages poorly. You know what doesn't? The real thing. "It was not just a little fire—it was like the fire," Ana de Armas said in a recent interview. "And it was practical. We did it. The fire, the water—everything." =

The storyline propping up Ballerina is some of the most basic movie stuff you'll ever see. When Eve (Ana de Armas) was a young girl, her father was killed by an evil cult of murderers (led by The Chancellor, played by Gabriel Byrne). Her entire life led her on a path of revenge to eventually take on The Cult and The Chancellor, and that leads her to, basically, two hours of highly-stylized, very violent action. The story is just a means to put the hypnotic action on the screen; the movie, like any in the John Wick franchise, features moment after moment of violence that simply grips your brain into not looking away.

Most of the action throughout the film is similar to what we've seen in other Wick movies. But near the end, Eve picks up a flamethrower, locking herself in a duel with The Chancellor's chief henchman, Dex (Robert Maaser), and that's when the movie really does something special. These two trained killers spend much of the next 15 minutes firing blazes of glory at each other; fire everywhere, the stakes sky high knowing that one false move will turn either character into the burnt end of a matchstick.

Eventually, the scene concludes when Eve brandishes a firehose, and the eternal battle of fire vs. water becomes our latest showdown.
