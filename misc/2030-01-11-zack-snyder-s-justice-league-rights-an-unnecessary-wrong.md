# ‘Zack Snyder’s Justice League’ Rights an Unnecessary Wrong

In what turns out to be the actual final scene among many final scenes in Zack Snyder’s Justice League, Ben Affleck as Bruce Wayne gets out of bed. The sepia-filtered sunrise illuminates his lakefront window house. He’s wearing a black crewneck and black yoga pants (retired vigilante nightwear). Bruce goes outside, where he meets Martian Manhunter. I did not really understand what was happening here, both because I did not understand what was happening for most of the Snyder Cut, but also because I was distracted by how well Ben Affleck was lit, and how thrilled he seemed to be there.

This is the film’s quietest scene. It contains none of the blood and gore and cursing and time-consuming slow-motion that defines Snyder’s overwhelming (though truly unique) aesthetic, but it represents the Snyder Cut best. Here, Affleck is giving his all. He shouldn’t be up for an Oscar or anything, but he’s truly happy to be playing Snyder’s Bruce Wayne and, as a filmmaker himself, he’s happy to help execute another filmmaker’s vision.

The Snyder Cut of Justice League is a much better movie than Joss Whedon’s 2017 butchered sequence of scenes (it would be too generous to call it a movie) because it is fun, absurd, and dramatic, and the story and its characters are given context and emotional investment. The two versions are completely different in plot, character, and style, but the most significant differences are its performances, particularly those of Ben Affleck, Ray Fisher, and Gal Gadot. Their wooden, uncomfortable, insipid performances in Joss Whedon’s Justice League are in stark contrast with their warm, enthusiastic, and compelling performances in the Snyder Cut. It is no coincidence, then, that all three actors were early champions of the Snyder Cut, and encouraged the campaign for its release.

In July 2020, Ray Fisher called Whedon’s behavior on set “gross, abusive, unprofessional and completely unacceptable,” prompting an investigation at WarnerMedia. In December 2020, Gadot said her experience with Whedon “wasn’t the best,” and said she reported her experience to the “higher ups.” After the investigation, WarnerMedia said in a statement that “remedial action” had been taken. Since, Whedon stepped away from the HBO series The Nevers due to being “genuinely exhausted.” In February 2021, Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Angel star Charisma Carpenter posted a statement on social media detailing her experience with Whedon, who she says abused his power and wrote her off Angel right after she gave birth. Other stars of Whedon’s shows, including Sarah Michelle Gellar, Michelle Tratchenberg and David Borneanaz, publicly posted their support.

Justice League should have made Ray Fisher a star; instead, it made him an activist for himself against a major movie studio and a major filmmaker who mistreated him. Fisher took a major role in a major studio film in which a Black superhero was not only a main character, but the film’s emotional anchor with the clearest, most-compelling character arc. But when Snyder left after the death of his daughter, Whedon swooped in and reduced Cyborg to Bland Computer Man, reducing Cyborg’s story to one scene. Fisher's performance felt as strained as Cyborg’s storyline. His visible frustration in Whedon’s version is heartbreaking, and his enthusiasm in the Snyder Cut is essential.

Wonder Woman, Gal Gadot's debut solo movie as Diana Prince, came out several months prior to Justice League in 2017, and was a promising departure from the dreary, nonsensical DCEU films. Even if Justice League was bad, at least she would be in it. Unfortunately, Gadot’s performance and role had little impact—she said her lines, she did the thing where she puts her hands together, but not much else. In Justice League, Gadot didn’t have any of Diana’s warmth, because all of that was stripped away by Whedon, who valued shots of her ass more than the character’s development and personality. Snyder does not have Patty Jenkins’ touch, but he follows through on her perspective of the character (despite the fact that Diana turns Roose Bolton into dust in front of a bunch of kids), and it shows in Gadot’s confident performance. It's one far more cohesive with the Jenkins Wonder Woman films.

The Snyder Cut is more than just a director’s cut. The better performances in it prove that Affleck, Fisher, Gadot, Henry Cavill, Ezra Miller and Jason Momoa did not like what happened to the film they signed up for, and had already shot, especially given the circumstances in which Snyder had to exit. The actors knew releasing the Snyder Cut would rewrite the film’s history, secure Snyder’s legacy, and put Whedon’s behavior and motives into question. I will probably never love a Zack Snyder film—his movies feel like music videos that exist in a video game (or video games that exist in a music video, it works both ways)—but the Snyder Cut left me with more respect for him as an artist, and I kind of liked it maybe? Snyder’s cinematic vomit vibe is specific and not for everyone, but he has passion for the source material, for movies, and for the actors.

The journey to the Snyder Cut release was nauseating at best. But it is absolutely necessary, especially given Fisher’s accusations against Whedon. The Snyder Cut tells the story of superheroes who put their differences aside in order to save the world from bad guys. The real story of the Snyder Cut, though, is one of a guy who went out of his way to ruin a vision he was hired to finish under tragic circumstances, and the actors who banded together to ensure the right—and, ultimately, better—version saw the light of day. Through a sepia filter, of course.
