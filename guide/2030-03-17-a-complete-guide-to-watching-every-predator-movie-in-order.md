# A Complete Guide to Watching Every 'Predator' Movie In Order

BY 1987, ROCKY Balboa had vanquished a succession of more and more improbable foes. He’d defeated Clubber Lang, the indomitable, brutal product of the Chicago streets, played by Mr. T. He’d gone from Philly underdog to the pride of a nation when he’d beaten a Russian twice his size, sculpted by the best training equipment and anabolic steroids money can buy in Ivan Drago, played by Dolph Lundgren. He kind of beat up Thunder Lips, the Ultimate Male, a professional wrestling champion and very normal guy portrayed by totally normal guy Hulk Hogan. It took two tries, but he’d beaten the heavyweight champion Apollo Creed, played by Carl Weathers. The joke went, if they were to make a fifth Rocky film, the only plausible challenger left for the Italian Stallion was an unkillable super predator from outer space. Some Hollywood barstool historians would have you believe this idea is what led to the eventual birth of John McTiernan’s 1987 classic, Predator.

It’s a legendary article of Hollywood lore that one of the best, longest-lasting cinematic action franchise properties of the last four decades was the product of a cynical joke. It’s also apparently not true, if series creators Jim and John Thomas are to be believed (Though you can understand why they might be motivated to disprove the notion that their legacy was essentially a dare). Their assertion is they began work on a script they called Hunter in 1983—two years before the release of Rocky IV—about a league of big-game alien trackers coming to Earth to kill humans for sport. But there’s also a kind of poetic resonance, an emotional truth, at the core of the urban legend.

Predator was the product of the sequel-and-series-mad cocaine '80s, full of endless horror and action installments and original films that felt like extensions of existing franchises, movies whose premises played like elevator pitches the writer and director hadn’t figured out much more of since they’d cooked them up after a three martini lunch. There are many bad versions of movies like Predator—and Predator itself easily could’ve been a far worse film.

Director John McTiernan wasn’t John McTiernan yet—this was only his second film, before he’d go on to make Die Hard, The Hunt For Red October, and The Thomas Crown Affair. His lead, Arnold Schwarzenegger, was only beginning to build his legend as one of the box office phenomenon superstars of his generation. They went to set—outside Puerto Vallarta, where Arnie would almost immediately contract a brutal case of food poisoning—without a finished script, and with a young Jean Claude Van Damme slated to play the titular alien (he’d soon be fired and replaced by the great Kevin Peter Hall). They also arrived with neither a firm idea of what the Predator would eventually look like nor an inkling of how to execute the visual cues—the Predator’s invisible camouflage effect or the infrared heat vision—that would become hallmarks of the franchise. The masterpiece of summer popcorn cinema that ultimately resulted is a monument to the miracle of movie making, a triumph of ingenuity and resourcefulness born with guns of budget and time and technology pressed to the collective temple of the production. Predator was the birth of several legends above and below the line that minted generational fortunes and caught America entirely by surprise. It sounds like an off the cuff joke told over shots and a few bathroom trips at a Hollywood bar, even if a literal joke wasn’t actually the source IP.

The resulting Predator franchise, unfurled over four decades, was initially about the anxieties surrounding incompetent, corrupt U.S. government and politics of the era. But when the franchise resumed following 1990's Predator 2, after a 14-year break with the first installment of Alien Vs. Predator, it settles into the groove of dumb, fun films that split the difference between action and horror and really aren’t about much more than survivalist thrills. Each film follows the same basic structure, there’s a battle between two human factions, be it soldiers vs narcos, or gangs vs cops, or Viking warriors, or samurais, or Comanches. The predator observes, then begins picking off the befuddled enemy combatants, culling wheat from chaff before a showdown with the ultimate warrior.

Predators are closer to humanoid than the indestructible Xenomorph beasts that they're so often paired with both on-screen and in Hollywood history conversation. Predators are a gladiatorial species of hyper-intelligent monsters with deadly technology that far outpaces ours, are physically superior to man, and are ruthless, killing purely for the challenge and the fun (with a warped code of conduct). Yet somehow, whether armed with AR-15s or primitive spears, at least one human manages to best these killing machines in each installment.

The Predator mythology has remained a moving target for nearly 40 years, across installments that have been made by many hands and competing visions that thought they represented fresh starts and new directions, before failing to launch (despite always bringing in a tidy sum)

But that is all changing. Quietly, over the past five years, Dan Trachtenberg, a ronin who once wandered the desert of IP genre filmmaking, has taken stewardship of the Predator-verse, returning for a third bite at the apple with Predator: Badlands, the series's official seventh installment (depending on how you qualify the two AVPs). Trachtenberg has gone back to the literal dawn of several civilizations to begin building toward a coherent, unified theory of Predator that braids the multiple strands of story left hanging by directors John McTiernan, and Stephen Hopkins, and Paul W.S. Anderson, and Shane Black and…..Nimród Antal? (but ideally NOT Greg and Colin Strause). His plan is to form a legible galaxy, a humming machinery of “big story” within which a modern franchise can be built.

Alien Vs. Predator dir. Paul W.S. Anderson (2004)

In the proud tradition of Renny Harlin’s Deep Blue Sea, which builds its plot around the desperate mission to cure Alzheimer's by creating a super race of smart sharks, this mash-up from the dawn of the nerd fan-service film posits Predators were actually ancient aliens who have been visiting Earth for thousands of years and that they built the pyramids and helped man develop civilization.

Charles Weyland (of Weyland-Yutani, the evil corporation in the Alien franchise pursuing the eternal folly of harnessing Xenomorphs as biotech) sends a team to investigate a pyramid that has been discovered under the ice off the coast of Antarctica, guided by Lex Woods (Sanaa Lathan, worth the price of entry). The team finds out the pyramid has long served as a hunting ground, where humans are ritually sacrificed as hosts for face-huggers to breed Xenomorphs, whom the Predators must then hunt as a kind of Predator Bar Mitzvah.

Traces of Xenomorphs have been showing up in Predator films dating back to 2 in 1990. This crossover event is the odd case of story that is non-canonical in the Alien franchise but canonical to Predator, so it’s a good place to start if you want to understand the history of the Predators on Earth.

Prey dir. Dan Trachtenberg (2022)

The Trachtenberg era begins by going back in time as well as back to basics. It’s the early 18th century in the Great Plains of the central territories of North America and a Comanche girl named Naru sets out on a rite of passage not so different from the hunting exhibitions we see the Predators partake in across films. It’s a quiet, elemental, triumphant film that resets the franchise by reminding us of its base pleasures (and shows us the origin story of a pistol that will appear in Predator 2).

Predator dir. John McTiernan (1987)

A crack team of Vietnam veterans descends into a Central American jungle, convinced they are in one kind of movie—like Commando or Rambo: First Blood Part II—only to discover they are in a very different film. This sets the brilliant template: a team of perfectly cast, overqualified character actors, layers of intrigue, and the shock of seeing the perfect killer alien for the first time. There is no careful road paving for a franchise. We don’t learn much about the Predator, what it wants, or where it's from, but that’s information we don’t need, exposition that would bog this Swiss watch down. It’s still an elemental masterpiece whose genius hasn’t been dulled at all by subsequent chapters, and still the best Predator by far.

Predator 2 (1990)

This movie, on the other hand, is so fucking deranged. It's been semi-lost to time (at least relative to the first film), which is a shame because it's fascinating. Predator 2 is closer to a horror flick with a vibe that feels more out of the late '80s than the early '90s, partially due to some really entertaining tough cop talk performances from Danny Glover, Gary Busey, and Bill Paxton (who was the only actor to appear in both the Alien and Predator franchises independently).

The film is set in 1997, so that puts events nearly a decade into “the future,” but it's portrayed like a society on the edge of dystopian collapse. We open in the middle of a warzone, where a shootout is taking place in the sweltering streets of Los Angeles. The LAPD is engaging with a coke-fueled, culturally insensitively dressed, ritual murdering paramilitary organization called King Willie’s Voodoo Gang.

The Predator in this film says "motherfucker" in English, refuses to kill a pregnant woman, and gives us the beginning of a sense of Predator society.

The Predator dir. Shane Black (2018)

Shane Black returns to a franchise he helped launch (as an actor in the 1987 original, although he also helped punch the script up on set) 31 years later—this time as the co-writer and director. This is the first attempt to really build up the franchise's lore, and Black throws a lot at the wall. Predators are constantly experimenting on themselves, kidnapping warriors and taking their DNA to splice with their own in order to speed evolution and turn themselves into hybrid superbeings. There are Predator dogs. Predators are anticipating the extinction of life on Earth, and with their love of extreme high temperatures are preparing to take over our planet. The inciting action is that a Predator Benedict Arnold has crashed on Earth with tech that can save humanity. His arrival brings the next step in Predator evolution, an 11-foot-tall tracker, to our planet to put him down. And perhaps most incredibly: the film posits that autism is the next step in human evolution; The Predators want Boyd Holbrook's character’s autistic son to bring back to their home planet, so they can study and steal DNA from him.

It’s Shane Black (who also was behind The Nice Guys and Iron Man 3), so there’s some elite banter, and shots of Cuervo, and Olivia Munn as an alien biologist, and Sterling K. Brown as a wiseass evil heavy, and we’re on the precipice of a holiday (Halloween in this case, not his traditional Christmas). There's lots of good and fun stuff here, but it’s also kind of a mess.

Predator: Badlands dir. Dan Trachtenberg (2025)

Trachtenberg’s latest is the most radical of the Predator films to date. He chooses a Yautja (the proper name for what we generally know as Predators) with a name that sounds like a legendary graf tag (Dek) as his protagonist. Dek never wears a mask and never goes invisible, an adequate metaphor for the vulnerability Patrick Aison’s screenplay imbues him with. We see Dek tend to his own wounds, crack jokes, express hurt at betrayal and concern for his friends, and even smile at one point, I think.

So it makes sense that Dek does not get along with the Darwinian dickhead warrior culture that governs Yautja society on his home planet. He breaks from his ruthless father and goes out to prove himself as worthy, fighting his way through an alien death planet chock full of Jim Henson-worthy creatures (both cute and scary), as well as Weyland-Yutani’s biotech cyborg research army forces, only to realize his father's approval is not something he's interested in gaining.

Badlands is placed where it is in this chronology because of a hunch I have based on a tossed off line from 2010’s Predators. There is mention of tribal war on the home planet between an army of small predators and an army of larger predators. Dek’s defining trait is he’s a runt, and at the end of the film declares he’s forming his own found family apart from existing Yautja culture and customs. If I’m right, it’s savvy franchise building on Trachtenberg’s part, smoothly inserting this film into the middle of the Predator timeline (circa 2179, based on the Weyland-Yutani tech on display that bears a striking resemblance to shit you see in James Cameron’s Aliens) I’d guess Dek is a kind of Caesar figure from Rupert Wyatt and Matt Reeves’s Planet of the Apes trilogy, and this film was the first chapter in a Civil War that takes place years if not generations before...

Predators takes the show on the road (after the action of the first Predator, we’re informed early in the film), to the Predators home planet/game preserve to give us a glimpse of their native habitat. They don’t just play road games, but also will abduct warriors from other planets to hunt in their backyard. We also get the first sense there is Predator vs. Predator internecine conflict.

Predator: Killer of Killers dir. Dan Trachtenberg (2025)

It was difficult to know where to place this series of animated vignettes in the chronology because it goes way further back than even Prey (to 9th century Scandinavia, Edo Period Japan, and a WWII airbattle, but also jumps ahead and brings in lore from the preceding films).

Three combatants from history face off with Predators and come out victorious. They’re all eventually kidnapped from their homes and time, and transported to Predator world where they’re fitted with exploding collars and instructed to fight to the death so the winner can be decapitated by a Julius Caesar general/king/giant bone warrior type.

In the end, we discover Predators have a Raiders of the Lost Ark-style storage facility where they keep all the greatest warriors in hyperbaric cryosleep pods—and that includes Naru, Danny Glover, and Arnold Schwarzenegger. This opens the door for a cross-pollinated single lore narrative.

Aliens Vs. Predator: Requiem dir. The Brothers Strause (2007)

I put this at the end, even though it immediately follows the ending of the first AVP installment—it contributes nothing of substance to the greater Predator narrative. In discussing the first Predator I mentioned that it was brilliant because it was concentrated, self-contained tension. Here, it makes for a big nothing burger, with none of the consideration or brilliant invention that made that first film a classic. This movie is also shot without light and you literally can’t see what’s happening on screen most of the time. Feel free to skip!
