What scleroderma actually is and how it starts

Scleroderma means "hard skin," and that's exactly what happens at the cellular level. Your immune system mistakenly activates fibroblasts, the cells responsible for producing collagen. Instead of stopping when they should, these cells keep producing collagen, leading to progressive thickening and tightening of the skin. This isn't a surface problem. It's a systemic process driven by immune dysregulation, vascular damage, and uncontrolled fibrosis.

The condition exists on a spectrum. Localized scleroderma affects only the skin and underlying tissues, typically appearing as patches or streaks of thickened skin. Systemic sclerosis, the more severe form, extends beyond the skin to affect blood vessels and internal organs including the lungs, heart, kidneys, and digestive tract. The distinction matters because the types of scleroderma follow different trajectories and require different monitoring strategies.

What triggers this cascade isn't fully understood, but the process begins with blood vessel injury. Damaged endothelial cells release inflammatory signals that recruit immune cells. These immune cells, in turn, release cytokines and growth factors that activate fibroblasts. Once activated, fibroblasts produce excessive amounts of collagen and other extracellular matrix proteins, creating the characteristic skin thickening and organ fibrosis.

Types of scleroderma and what they mean

Localized scleroderma

Localized scleroderma primarily affects the skin without involving internal organs. Morphea, the most common type, presents as waxy, discolored patches on the chest, back, stomach, arms, or legs. Linear scleroderma appears as streaks of thickened skin, often on the arms or legs, and can affect underlying muscle and bone when it occurs in children. While localized forms don't typically threaten organ function, they can cause significant cosmetic concerns and, in linear forms affecting the face or limbs, functional limitations and joint contractures.

Systemic sclerosis

Systemic sclerosis divides into two main subtypes based on the extent of skin involvement:

Limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis affects the skin of the fingers, hands, forearms, feet, lower legs, and face, progressing slowly (previously known as CREST syndrome: Calcinosis, Raynaud's phenomenon, Esophageal dysmotility, Sclerodactyly, Telangiectasia).

Diffuse cutaneous systemic sclerosis involves more widespread skin thickening that can affect the trunk and upper arms, tending to progress more rapidly with a higher risk of early internal organ involvement, particularly affecting the lungs, heart, and kidneys.

Systemic sclerosis sine scleroderma presents with internal organ involvement without significant skin thickening, accounting for about 5% of cases and proving particularly challenging to diagnose.

Scleroderma face changes and skin symptoms

Scleroderma face changes are among the most visible and distressing manifestations of the disease. The skin becomes tight, shiny, and loses its natural elasticity. The face may appear mask-like with reduced expression. The mouth opening becomes progressively smaller (microstomia), which can interfere with eating, dental care, and speech. The nose may become pinched and smaller. Vertical lines appear around the mouth as the skin tightens.

These facial changes develop gradually over months to years as collagen accumulates in the dermis. The skin may feel itchy during active phases of inflammation, and some patients notice their skin becoming darker or lighter in affected areas. Telangiectasias (small dilated blood vessels visible on the skin surface) commonly appear on the face, hands, and chest.

How scleroderma connects to immune function, vascular health, and inflammation

Scleroderma is fundamentally an immune-mediated disease. Both innate and adaptive immune responses are dysregulated. T cells, B cells, and macrophages infiltrate affected tissues and release pro-fibrotic cytokines including transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-beta), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and interleukin-4 (IL-4). These cytokines activate fibroblasts and promote their transformation into myofibroblasts, which produce even more collagen and contract, contributing to tissue stiffness.

Vascular injury is an early and persistent feature. Endothelial cells lining blood vessels become damaged, leading to increased vascular permeability, platelet activation, and impaired blood flow. This vascular dysfunction manifests as Raynaud's phenomenon, where fingers and toes turn white, then blue, then red in response to cold or stress. Over time, chronic vascular damage leads to structural changes in small blood vessels, reducing blood supply to tissues and contributing to digital ulcers and, in severe cases, pulmonary arterial hypertension.

The gut-skin axis also plays a role. Emerging research suggests that gut microbiome composition differs in scleroderma patients compared to healthy individuals, with reduced microbial diversity and altered bacterial populations. Intestinal permeability may allow bacterial products to enter circulation, triggering systemic inflammation that amplifies skin and organ fibrosis. Gastrointestinal symptoms are common in systemic sclerosis, affecting up to 90% of patients, and include reflux, dysphagia, bloating, and altered bowel function.

What drives flares and progression

Scleroderma progression varies widely between individuals, but certain factors influence disease activity and severity:

Stress activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, increasing cortisol production and potentially impairing skin barrier function, increasing mast cell activation, and worsening vascular reactivity.

Environmental exposures (silica dust, organic solvents, certain medications) have been linked to scleroderma development or worsening.

Smoking damages blood vessels and worsens vascular complications.

Cold exposure triggers Raynaud's phenomenon and can precipitate digital ulcers in patients with compromised circulation.

Hormonal factors influence disease expression, with scleroderma being more common in women during childbearing years.

Infections and immune triggers can precipitate flares, with viral infections potentially activating the immune system in ways that amplify autoimmune responses.

Why the same condition looks different in different people

Genetic factors shape scleroderma susceptibility and phenotype. Certain HLA gene variants increase risk, and family members of scleroderma patients have higher rates of autoimmune diseases. Filaggrin gene mutations, which affect skin barrier function, may influence skin involvement severity. Genetic variations in collagen metabolism, immune signaling pathways, and vascular function all contribute to individual differences in disease presentation.

Autoantibody profiles predict disease subtype and prognosis. Anti-centromere antibodies are associated with limited cutaneous disease and a lower risk of severe internal organ involvement. Anti-topoisomerase I (Scl-70) antibodies correlate with diffuse cutaneous disease and increased risk of interstitial lung disease. Anti-RNA polymerase III antibodies are linked to diffuse disease with rapid skin progression and increased risk of scleroderma renal crisis. These antibody patterns help clinicians anticipate which organs may be affected and guide monitoring strategies.

Skin microbiome composition varies between individuals and may influence local inflammation and barrier function. Patients with more severe skin involvement show different bacterial populations on affected skin compared to those with milder disease. Age at onset also affects disease trajectory, with younger patients (particularly those diagnosed before age 40) tending to have more aggressive disease with faster skin progression, while older patients may have slower progression but face complications related to age-related comorbidities.

What testing can show when skin changes persist

When scleroderma is suspected, antinuclear antibody (ANA) testing is the first step. ANA is positive in over 90% of systemic sclerosis cases. If ANA is positive, more specific antibody testing follows, including anti-centromere, anti-Scl-70, and anti-RNA polymerase III antibodies. These scleroderma-specific antibodies help classify disease subtype and predict organ involvement patterns.

Inflammatory markers provide context. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) may be elevated during active inflammation, though they're not specific to scleroderma. Tracking these markers over time can help assess disease activity and response to treatment.

Organ-specific testing is essential for systemic sclerosis. Pulmonary function tests and high-resolution chest CT detect interstitial lung disease. Echocardiography screens for pulmonary hypertension and cardiac involvement. Kidney function monitoring, including creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), is critical, especially in patients at risk for renal crisis.

Nutritional and metabolic markers matter when gastrointestinal involvement is present. Albumin, vitamin B12, folate, and vitamin D levels can reveal malabsorption. Ferritin and iron studies assess for anemia, which can result from chronic inflammation or gastrointestinal bleeding.

Scleroderma medication and management approaches

There is no single scleroderma medication that stops collagen overproduction entirely, and treatment focuses on managing symptoms, slowing progression, and preventing organ damage:

Methotrexate is often used off-label as first-line systemic therapy for localized scleroderma with severe skin or musculoskeletal involvement.

Immunosuppressants (mycophenolate mofetil and cyclophosphamide) are used in systemic sclerosis, particularly when interstitial lung disease is present.

Calcium channel blockers improve blood flow and reduce vasospasm in Raynaud's phenomenon.

Phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors and endothelin receptor antagonists treat severe vascular complications including digital ulcers and pulmonary arterial hypertension.

ACE inhibitors are critical for preventing and treating scleroderma renal crisis.

Proton pump inhibitors reduce reflux, prokinetic agents improve esophageal motility, and antibiotics treat small intestinal bacterial overgrowth.

Nutritional support includes supplementation with vitamin D, B vitamins, and iron to address deficiencies from malabsorption.

Emerging therapies target specific pathways in scleroderma pathogenesis. Antifibrotic agents are being studied for their ability to reduce collagen production. Biologics targeting specific cytokines or immune cells show promise in clinical trials. The 2024 British Society for Rheumatology and EULAR guidelines now include recommendations for synthetic and biological targeted therapies for fibrotic manifestations of systemic sclerosis, reflecting advances in treatment options.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is scleroderma and how does it affect the skin?

Scleroderma means hard skin, and the name describes the central process: the immune system mistakenly activates fibroblasts, which produce excess collagen instead of stopping at normal levels. Progressive thickening and tightening of the skin results. Beyond the skin, this fibrotic process can extend to blood vessels and internal organs. Visible skin changes include tightness, shininess, reduced elasticity, and structural changes to the face and hands in later stages.

What is the difference between localized scleroderma and systemic sclerosis?

Localized scleroderma affects only the skin and underlying tissues, appearing as patches or streaks of thickened skin without involving internal organs. Systemic sclerosis extends beyond the skin to affect blood vessels and internal organs including the lungs, heart, kidneys, and digestive tract. Within systemic sclerosis, limited cutaneous disease progresses slowly and affects the hands, face, and lower arms, while diffuse cutaneous disease is more widespread and progresses faster with earlier organ involvement.
