Explanation

Jumper's Knee Relief: Platelet-Rich Plasma Protocols

Jumper's knee, medically known as patellar tendonitis, is a common overuse injury among athletes and active individuals. Characterized by pain at the front of the knee—usually just below the kneecap—it can limit jumping, running and even everyday activities. Traditional treatments (rest, ice, physical therapy) help many, but some people need more advanced options. Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) has emerged as a promising therapy for chronic cases.

What Is PRP for Patellar Tendonitis?PRP is a concentration of your own platelets suspended in a small volume of plasma. Platelets contain growth factors that may accelerate healing in damaged tendons. Using a sample of your blood, a technician separates platelets via centrifugation, creating an injection that's up to five times richer in growth factors than whole blood.

Why Consider PRP?

Targets underlying tendon degeneration, not just pain.

Uses your own blood—minimal risk of allergic reaction.

Can be done in an outpatient setting.

Reported to reduce pain and improve function when conservative care fails.

Initial Consultation

Confirm diagnosis with clinical exam and, if needed, ultrasound or MRI.

Preparation Day

Avoid anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) 5–7 days before treatment, as they may blunt platelet activity.

Stay hydrated and eat a light meal.

Blood Draw & Processing

Draw 30–60 mL of your blood via standard venipuncture.

Spin in a centrifuge for 10–15 minutes.

Extract platelet-rich layer under sterile conditions.

Injection

Clean skin and apply local anesthetic.

Under ultrasound guidance, inject PRP directly into areas of tendon degeneration.

The procedure takes 20–30 minutes.

Post-Injection Care

Rest the knee for 24–48 hours (light activity only).

Avoid NSAIDs for at least two weeks; use acetaminophen for discomfort.

Ice for 10–15 minutes as needed.

Follow-Up Injections

Most protocols call for 1–3 injections spaced 4–6 weeks apart.

Your specialist will tailor the number based on symptom severity and ultrasound findings.

What the Research Shows

A 2019 meta-analysis found PRP improved pain scores and function in chronic patellar tendonitis more than placebo or corticosteroid injections.

Randomized trials report 60–80% of patients achieving significant relief by 12 weeks post-injection.

Weeks 1–2: Rest and gentle range-of-motion exercises.

Weeks 3–6: Begin guided physical therapy focusing on eccentric quadriceps loading and gradual strengthening.

Who's a Good Candidate?

Chronic patellar tendonitis lasting >3 months despite conservative care.

No active systemic infection or blood disorders.

Willingness to commit to post-injection rehabilitation.

Realistic about gradual recovery—full benefit may take up to 6 months.
