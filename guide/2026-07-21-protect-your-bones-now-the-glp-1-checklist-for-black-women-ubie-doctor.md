As you embark on or continue GLP-1 therapy for weight management or diabetes, it's vital to protect and strengthen your bones. While GLP-1 receptor agonists (like semaglutide or liraglutide) offer impressive metabolic benefits, studies suggest they can be linked to modest reductions in bone density if key precautions aren't taken. Below is a clear, evidence-based bone health protocol tailored for Black women on GLP-1s, grounded in current clinical guidelines and expert consensus.

1. Why Bone Health Matters on GLP-1 Therapy

• GLP-1 medications promote weight loss by reducing appetite and slowing gastric emptying.• Significant weight loss, if unaccompanied by proper bone-supportive measures, may accelerate bone density loss.• Maintaining strong bones lowers your risk of fractures, supports mobility, and preserves quality of life.

2. Unique Considerations for Black Women

Although Black women generally have higher peak bone mass than other groups, they still face:

• Later diagnosis of osteoporosis (often after a fracture).• Underdiagnosis and undertreatment of bone disease.• Potential for inclusions of undetected vitamin D deficiency due to melanin's effect on skin synthesis.

Being proactive with bone health protocols ensures you maximize the natural advantages you may have, while mitigating any new risks from therapy-related weight changes.

3. Core Components of Your Bone Health Protocol

A. Nutrition: Build a Strong Foundation

Adequate intake of key nutrients fuels bone formation and maintenance.

Calcium• Aim for 1,000–1,200 mg daily through diet or supplements.• Food sources: low-fat dairy (yogurt, cheese), fortified plant milks, leafy greens (kale, collards), sardines.

Vitamin D• Target 1,000–2,000 IU daily; check your blood 25(OH)D level every 6–12 months.• Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel), egg yolks, fortified cereals.

Protein• 1.0–1.2 g per kg body weight helps preserve muscle and support bone matrix.• Lean meats, beans, lentils, Greek yogurt, tofu.

Other Micronutrients• Magnesium, vitamin K, phosphorus, and trace minerals (zinc, copper) also contribute.• Eat a varied diet rich in whole grains, nuts, seeds, and fruits.

B. Exercise: The Non-Negotiable Habit

Mechanical stress from exercise signals bones to build and retain density.

Weight-Bearing Aerobics• Brisk walking, dancing, stair climbing—150 minutes per week.

Resistance Training• 2–3 sessions weekly focusing on major muscle groups (squats, lunges, rows).

Balance and Flexibility• Tai chi, yoga, single-leg stands reduce fall risk.• Aim for daily practice of balance exercises.

C. Lifestyle Habits: Small Changes, Big Impact

Quit Smoking• Tobacco use accelerates bone loss; seek support to stop.

Limit Alcohol• No more than 1 drink per day; excessive intake interferes with calcium absorption and hormonal balance.

Fall Prevention• Remove tripping hazards at home, ensure proper lighting, wear supportive footwear, and use grab bars if needed.

D. Medical Monitoring: Stay Ahead of Changes

Bone Density Scans (DEXA)• Baseline DEXA before or within the first year of significant GLP-1–induced weight loss.• Repeat every 2–3 years if risk factors persist or bone density is low.

Laboratory Tests• Serum 25(OH)D, calcium, phosphorus, parathyroid hormone (PTH) every 6–12 months.• Consider thyroid and kidney function tests if abnormalities arise.

Medication Review• Discuss with your doctor whether bone-protective agents (bisphosphonates, denosumab, or selective estrogen receptor modulators) are indicated.

E. Supplements & Adjunct Therapies

Calcium + Vitamin D Combination• If dietary intake is inadequate, choose supplements providing no more than 500 mg calcium per dose (better absorption).

Vitamin K2• Supports calcium deposition in bone rather than arteries; 90–120 mcg daily if recommended.

Emerging Treatments• Talk with your doctor about newer agents like romosozumab if you have osteoporosis and multiple risk factors.
